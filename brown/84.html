No. 84: Cam and double rack frame

A turning cam can push a frame left, push it right, or leave it alone. Which one happens depends only on how high the frame hangs. An engine used this to open or close its steam valve a little at a time.

How it works

The cam turns in the gap between the two rows of teeth. When the frame hangs in the middle, the pegs miss both rows. The cam spins and the frame stays still.

Raise the rod and the lower row of teeth comes up into the pegs' path. The pegs pass the lowest point of the circle moving right, so each one pushes the frame one tooth to the right. Lower the rod and the upper row meets the pegs instead. At the highest point of the circle they move left, so the frame goes left. When a row runs out of teeth, the pegs miss it and the frame stops at the end of its slot.

The model opens with the rod raised. Count: four pegs move the frame four teeth for each turn of the cam. Then try Middle, then Lowered. In an engine a speed governor lifted or lowered the rod, and the frame opened or closed the steam valve.

In Brown's words

The double rack-frame, B, is suspended from the rod, A. Continuous rotary motion is given to the cam, D. When the shaft of the cam is midway between the two racks, the cam acts upon neither of them; but by raising or lowering the rod, A, either the lower or upper rack is brought within range of the cam, and the rack-frame moved to the left or right. This movement has been used in connection with the governor of an engine, the rod, A, being connected with the governor, and the rack-frame with the throttle or regulating valve.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the cam as a spoked wheel, so here each arm carries a peg that reaches the teeth. The posts, the base, the top beam that guides the rod, the shaft's stand and all sizes and tooth counts were made up. The rows of teeth are kept short so that they run out just as the frame reaches the ends of its slot.
