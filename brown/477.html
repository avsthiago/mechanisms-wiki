No. 477: Diaphragm steam trap

Steam in a heating pipe slowly turns into water, and the water must be let out. This box lets the water out but keeps the steam in. A sealed valve full of liquid swells when steam reaches it and shrinks when water does.

How it works

Steam comes in at the top. While steam fills the box, the valve is pushed up against the ring and nothing can get out. Water that comes in collects in the top pipe, around the valve's stem. Watch it rise.

The liquid sealed in the valve grows when it is hot and shrinks when it cools. Hot, it bows the thin bottom down onto the bar and lifts the valve shut. Water is cooler than steam, so as water piles up round the stem the liquid shrinks. The valve sinks, and the water runs past it and out at the bottom. When steam follows, the valve warms and shuts again.

Try a little water, then a lot. Watch how often the valve opens. Choose Closed to see the trap as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Steam trap for shutting in steam, but providing for the escape of water from steam coils and radiators (Hoard & Wiggin's patent). It consists of a box, connected at A with the end of the coil or the waste-pipe, having an outlet at B, and furnished with a hollow valve, D, the bottom of which is composed of a flexible diaphragm. Valve is filled with liquid, and hermetically sealed, and its diaphragm rests upon a bridge over the outlet-pipe. The presence of steam in the outer box so heats the water in valve that the diaphragm expands and raises valve up to the seat, a, a. Water of condensation accumulating reduces the temperature of valve ; and as the liquid in valve contracts, diaphragm allows valve to descend and let water off.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front half is cut away so the inside shows, as in the engraving. The swelling of the bottom sheet and the movement of the valve are much bigger than in a real trap, so you can see them. The temperatures, timings and sizes are made up. The dots show the steam and the water.
