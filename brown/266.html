No. 266: Differential screw

One shaft carries two screws with threads of different spacing. Turning the shaft moves a sliding bearing by only the small difference between them. It gives a very fine, slow and strong push from an ordinary crank.

How it works

Turn the crank and the shaft screws itself through the fixed bearing, so the whole shaft slides along. The right screw turns inside the sliding bearing at the same time. The sliding bearing moves too, but much less than the shaft.

Each turn, the left screw carries the shaft forward by one wide thread space. In the same turn the right screw pulls the sliding bearing back by one narrow thread space. What is left over is the small difference between the two, so the bearing creeps.

Try Same thread and the sliding bearing stays still while the shaft moves. Then try A little finer and Much finer, and watch the red line.

In Brown's words

The shaft has two screws of different pitches cut on it, one screwing into a fixed bearing, and the other into a bearing free to move to and fro. Rotary motion of the shaft gives rectilinear motion to the movable bearing, a distance equal to the difference of pitches, at each revolution.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The thread spacings and sizes were made up. Brown does not show how the sliding bearing is kept from turning; here it just rests on the base. A real shaft can only screw so far, so the model turns the crank the other way at each end of its travel.
