No. 469: Rotary motion from warm water

An old French idea for getting turning motion from warm water. A screw in a cold tank pushes air down a pipe, and the bubbles rise under a wheel in a warm tank and turn it. The wheel turns the screw, and because warm air takes up more room than cold air, this is supposed to keep it going.

How it works

Follow the white bubbles. The screw in the cold tank pushes pockets of air down into the bell. The air climbs the pipe, crosses over, and bubbles out under the wheel in the warm tank. It is caught under the blades on the left and floats that side up, so the wheel turns. The wheel drives the screw through the gears.

Why might it keep going? Air swells when it warms. The screw pushes down cold air, but the wheel is lifted by the same air after it has warmed and grown. Bigger bubbles lift harder, so the wheel gets back a little more than it spends. This is really a heat engine, like a steam engine, and it only runs while one tank stays warmer than the other. Brown points out that nobody says how that is done. Rub in the parts takes most of the small gain.

Try a nearly boiling tank, then make it the same as the cold one. With no difference in warmth, the air does not grow and the wheel slows to a stop. Choose Closed to see the tube and bell as they look from outside.

In Brown's words

French invention for obtaining rotary motion from different temperatures in two bodies of water. Two cisterns contain water : that in left at natural temperature and that in right higher. In right is a water-wheel geared with Archimedean screw in left. From spiral screw of the latter a pipe extends over and passes to the under side of wheel. Machine is started by turning screw in opposite direction to that for raising water, thus forcing down air, which ascends in tube, crosses and descends, and imparts motion to wheel ; and its volume increasing with change of temperature, it is said, keeps the machine in motion. We are not informed how the difference of temperature is to be maintained.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's description and drawing. The supports for the shafts, the feet of the bell, the shape of the blades and all sizes were made up. The machine is drawn as Brown shows it; the speed it settles at is not worked out from real forces. Here it simply runs faster the more the warm air grows, after taking off a made-up share for rub, and stops when there is too little. When it has stopped and the tank is made warm again, the model gives the screw a turn by hand to start it, as Brown says it must be started.
