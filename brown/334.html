No. 334: Rack and segment parallel motion

A straight row of teeth, called a rack, is fixed to the piston rod. It meshes with a curved row of teeth on the rocking beam. As the beam rocks, it rolls the rack straight up and down, with no sideways swing at all.

How it works

Watch the curved row of teeth on the beam. It rolls along the straight rack like a wheel rolling along a track. The rack, and the piston rod below it, move straight up and down. Their red trace is a perfect line.

The teeth push sideways on the rack as well as along it. A roller presses on the back of the rack and takes that push, so the rack stays in mesh. The curved arch in the middle of the beam does the same job for the pump rod with a chain that winds on and off it.

The pump rod hangs closer to the beam's pin than the rack, so it moves a shorter way. Try a short swing and a long one, and compare how far each rod moves. Choose Closed to see the cylinder as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Shows a parallel motion used in some of the old single-acting beam engines. The piston-rod is formed with a straight rack gearing with a toothed segment on the beam. The back of the rack works against a roller, A.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown marks the arch but does not say what hangs from it; here it holds a pump rod on a chain. The cylinder, the floor, the legs under the cross beam, the post under the beam's pin and the bracket for the roller were added. The tooth size was chosen to suit the drawing. The drawing shows the beam at the top of its swing. It is rocked steadily, as the engine would rock it.
