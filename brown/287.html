No. 287: Spring governor

Two balls are fixed to the middle of two bowed steel springs that spin with a spindle. As the speed rises, the balls pull the springs further out. The springs get shorter from end to end, so they lift a sleeve that can work the engine's steam valve.

How it works

The engine turns the spindle. The springs and balls spin round with it. Spinning things try to fly outwards, so the balls pull the middle of each spring away from the spindle.

A spring cannot get longer. So when its middle bows further out, its two ends must come closer together. The top end is fixed, so the sleeve at the bottom is pulled up. When the engine slows, the springs straighten and push the sleeve back down. The ring and lever pass this up and down movement on to the steam valve.

Move the speed slider up and down and watch the balls and the sleeve. Then try soft springs. They let the balls fly out at a lower speed. Stiff springs hold them in until the engine runs faster.

In Brown's words

Pickering's governor. The balls are attached to springs the upper end of each of which is attached to a collar fixed on the spindle, and the lower end to a collar on the sliding sleeve. The springs yield in a proper degree to the centrifugal force of the balls, and raise the sleeve ; and as the centrifugal force diminishes, they draw the balls toward the spindle and depress the sleeve.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows two springs, so there are two here. The floor, the bearing, the ring, the lever and its post were made up. Each spring is drawn as half a wave whose length never changes. Its stiffness is worked out as if it only bends, which is close enough for small changes in its bow. The ball and sleeve weights, the spring strengths and the rub that calms the motion were chosen to look right. Stops keep the springs from going straight or bowing too far.
