No. 413: Adjustable friction gearing

Two wheels without teeth drive each other by rubbing. The upper wheel has a rubber edge squeezed between two metal plates. Tighten the nut and the rubber swells into the groove below, so it grips harder.

How it works

Turn the crank and the grooved wheel turns. The rubber edge of the upper wheel sits in its groove. Rubbing between the two carries the turn up, and the upper wheel goes the other way.

The upper wheel drives some machine, and that machine pushes back. If the rubber presses only lightly on the sides of the groove, it cannot carry that load. It slips and the upper wheel turns slower than it should. Screwing up the nut squeezes the rubber between the plates. It grows wider, wedges harder into the V, and grips.

Try Loose, then Half, then Tight, and watch the slip readout. Then turn the speed up: at Half the grip holds when it is slow and slips when it is fast.

In Brown's words

J. W. Howlett's patent adjustable frictional gearing. This is an improvement on that shown in 45 of this table. The upper wheel, A, shown in section, is composed of a rubber disk with V-edge, clamped between two metal plates. By screwing up the nut, B, which holds the parts together, the rubber disk is made to expand radially, and greater tractive power may be produced between the two wheels.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the upper wheel cut in half; here it is shown whole so you can see it turn. The upright, the base, the sizes and the angle of the V were made up. How hard the rubber grips and how hard the machine pushes back are made up too, chosen so the slip is easy to see. The rubber is drawn round all the way; really it would flatten a little where it presses into the groove.
