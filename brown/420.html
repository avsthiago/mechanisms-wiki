No. 420: Bell hammer

A tug on a wire swings a hammer against a bell. A spring under the hammer then lifts it clear. With the hammer off the metal, the bell can ring on.

How it works

Every few seconds the wire gets a sharp tug. The hammer flies at the bell and strikes it. Then it bounces back, and the spring catches it and holds it a little way off.

A bell rings because its metal shakes after the blow. Anything resting on it stops the shaking, just as a hand on a ringing glass stops it. The hammer is heavy and its weight pulls it towards the bell. The spring holds it up, so the head does not lie against the metal.

Try a weak spring. The hammer sinks onto the bell, and the ring dies at once. Try a stiff spring. The tug is no longer enough to reach the bell. The spring as drawn lets the hammer strike and come away.

In Brown's words

Arrangement of hammer for striking bells. Spring below the hammer raises it out of contact with the bell after striking, and so prevents it from interfering with the vibration of the metal in the bell.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not say how the wire is pulled; here it gets a sharp tug every three seconds. The beam the bell hangs from, the stop above the hammer, the weights and the strength of the tug were made up. The ring is shown by waves that fade as the bell goes quiet.
