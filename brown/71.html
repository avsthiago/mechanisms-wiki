No. 71: Tappet and studs with an inner locking rim

A wheel that turns all the time moves a second wheel on by one small step in each turn. Between steps two studs rest against the inside of a rim on the driving wheel, so the second wheel cannot drift. Counters use steps like this.

How it works

The driving wheel turns all the time. Three studs sit inside its rim. Once in each turn the arm on its back strikes the middle one and pushes it down. The stud slides out along the arm and drops off its round tip. By then the stud wheel has moved on by exactly one stud.

The rest of the time the upper and lower studs rest against the inside of the rim. The stud wheel cannot turn either way, because one of them would have to push out through the rim. The two gaps in the rim sit on each side of the arm. While the arm pushes, the lower stud leaves through one gap and a new stud comes in through the other.

So the stud wheel needs as many turns of the driver as it has studs to go round once. Try 8 studs, then 12, and watch how the rim gets bigger or smaller to fit.

In Brown's words

The inner circumference (shown by dotted lines) of the rim of the driving-wheel, B, serves as a lock against which two of the studs in the wheel, C, rest until the tappet, A, striking one of the studs, the next one below passes out from the guard-rim through the lower notch, and another stud enters the rim through the upper notch.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not give sizes, so the sizes of the wheels, the rim, the gaps and the arm, and the upright plate that holds them were made up. The driving wheel is see-through here, as Brown draws the rim in dotted lines. The arm has a round tip so it lets go of each stud gently. For a short time while a gap sweeps past a resting stud, the rim only holds the wheel one way. The choice of 8 or 12 studs was added to show what it changes.
