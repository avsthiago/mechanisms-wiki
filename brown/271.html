No. 271: Lever with two pawls on a ratchet bar

A lever rocks to and fro. Two small catches, called pawls, hang from it and hook the teeth of a long bar. One pawl pulls the bar along on the way up and the other pulls on the way down. So the bar keeps moving, almost without a pause.

How it works

Watch the lever rock about its pin. One pawl hangs from a point above the pin and the other from a point below it. When the lever turns, the two points move opposite ways: one goes left while the other goes right.

A pawl that is moved right hooks a tooth on its steep face and drags the bar along with it. At the same moment the other pawl is moved left. It slides up the long slope of a tooth and drops into the next gap. When the lever turns back, the two swap jobs. So the bar is pulled on both strokes and only pauses for an instant while the pawls change over.

Try the three swings. With a small swing each pawl pulls the bar only about one tooth. With a large swing it pulls several teeth, and the bar goes faster.

In Brown's words

On vibrating the lever to which the two pawls are attached, a nearly continuous rectilinear motion is given to the ratchet-bar.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows a bar on a table, a post with a lever, two long pawls and a reel. The table, post, base, clamps, pin positions, the size of the teeth and the lever swings were made up. The bar is shown as an endless strip: only its teeth and red marks slide along, so it never runs out. Brown's drawing does not say which way the bar moves. Here it moves to the right, pulling the strip off the reel.
