No. 161: Centrifugal governor

A spinning upright shaft carries two heavy balls on arms. The faster it spins, the further the balls fly out and the higher the sleeve below them rises. The sleeve moves a lever that shuts the steam valve a little, so the engine keeps a steady speed.

How it works

The engine turns a level shaft. Two bevel gears turn the upright spindle. The balls spin round with it. Spinning things try to fly outwards, so the balls swing away from the spindle and pull the arms up.

The links join the arms to a sleeve that slides on the spindle. When the balls swing out, the sleeve rises. A lever under it lifts a round plate in front of the steam pipe. Less steam reaches the engine, so it slows down. When it slows, the balls drop, the plate falls, and more steam flows.

Move the speed slider from slow to fast and watch the arm angle and the valve. Then try a heavy sleeve. It holds the balls down, so they need more speed to rise.

In Brown's words

Centrifugal governor for steam engines. The central spindle and attached arms and balls are driven from the engine by the bevel-gears at the top, and the balls fly out from the center by centrifugal force. If the speed of the engine increases, the balls fly out further from the center, and so raise the slide at the bottom and thereby reduce the opening of the regulating-valve which is connected with said slide. A diminution of speed produces an opposite effect.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the bevel gears face on, with the toothed face towards the viewer. Here the toothed face turns away so you can see the gears mesh. The stand, the wall, the bearings, the lever on its post, the link to the sleeve, the pipe and the round plate were made up. The ball weight, the arm lengths and the pull of gravity are chosen to look right. The sleeve weight choices were added for comparison.
