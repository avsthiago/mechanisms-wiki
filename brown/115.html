No. 115: Two pinions and a double rack

Two equal small gears, called pinions, sit one above the other, with their teeth locked together. A frame with teeth on its top and bottom edges surrounds them. When the pinions turn, both edges of the frame are pushed the same way, so the frame slides along evenly.

How it works

Rock the handle and the upper pinion turns. Its teeth push on the teeth of the lower pinion, so that one turns the opposite way. Each pinion pushes on its own row of teeth in the frame.

The top of one pinion and the bottom of the other move in the same direction. So both rows of teeth are pushed the same way, with equal force and equal speed. The frame stays straight and does not twist. The red stripes show that the pinions turn opposite ways, and the minus sign in the readout means left.

Try Short, then Medium, then Long. A longer turn of the pinions carries the frame further, in a straight line. Watch the frame and the readouts.

In Brown's words

Rotary motion of the toothed wheels produces rectilinear motion of the double rack and gives equal force and velocity to each side, both wheels being of equal size.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the gears, the oval frame and short rod ends. The handle, the back post, the guides for the rod ends, the base, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. The frame has rod ends so it can be guided. The pinions and the frame rock to and fro instead of turning round, and the choice of how far they turn was added.
