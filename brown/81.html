No. 81: Part-toothed gear and spring rack

A gear with teeth on only part of its rim lifts a toothed rod, then lets it go. A spring snaps the rod back down, ready for the next lift. A steady turn becomes a slow lift and a quick drop.

How it works

The gear turns clockwise all the time. While its teeth pass the rod, they hook into the rod's teeth and push the rod up. The spring above stretches as the rod rises.

Then the last tooth leaves the rod. The smooth part of the rim is too small to touch the rod's teeth, so nothing holds the rod up. The spring pulls it straight back down to its stop. It waits there until the first tooth comes round again and drops into the same gap as before.

The more teeth the gear has, the higher the rod goes and the shorter it rests. Try 3 teeth, then 7, and watch how high the rod climbs.

In Brown's words

Alternate rectilinear motion is given to the rack-rod, B, by the continuous revolution of the mutilated spur-gear, A, the spiral spring, C, forcing the rod back to its original position on the teeth of the gear, A, quitting the rack.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown's spring pushes the rod down; here it hangs from the top guide and pulls, which needs the same few parts. The plate behind, the guides, the stop under the rod, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. The choice of 3 or 5 teeth was added for comparison.
