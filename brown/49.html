No. 49: Ratchets turning one way from a rocking shaft

A level shaft rocks back and forth, like a pump handle. Two ratchets turn that rocking into steady turning of an upright shaft. The upright shaft always turns the same way, whichever way the handle swings.

How it works

The handle rocks the level shaft one way, then the other. Two sloping gears sit loose on that shaft, facing each other. Both fit into a third gear on the upright shaft. Each loose gear has a wheel with slanted teeth fixed to it, called a ratchet wheel.

An arm at each end of the rocking shaft carries a finger called a pawl. When the shaft swings one way, one pawl catches a ratchet tooth and pushes its gear round. The other pawl slides up the slanted teeth and clicks over them. When the shaft swings back, the pawls swap jobs. The two gears sit on opposite sides of the upright gear, so both pushes turn it the same way.

The pawl that is pushing turns red. After each change of direction there is a short wait while the pawl drops back onto a tooth. Try the Tiny swing. The pawls can no longer reach the next tooth, so the upright shaft stops. Then try Wide and compare.

In Brown's words

Alternate circular motion of the horizontal shaft produces a continuous rotary motion of the vertical shaft, by means of the ratchet-wheels secured to the bevel-gears, the ratchet-teeth of the two wheels being set opposite ways, and the pawls acting in opposite directions. The bevel-gears and ratchet-wheels are loose on the shaft, and the pawls attached to arms firmly secured on the shaft.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows no handle, so one was added to rock the shaft. The frame, the bar that holds the top of the upright shaft, the disc with the dark pointer, the sizes and the numbers of teeth were made up. A real upright shaft would coast a little between pushes. Here it stops at once.
