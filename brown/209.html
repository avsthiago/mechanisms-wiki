No. 209: Rolling contact with guide teeth

Two oval wheels turn each other by rolling, edge on edge, with no teeth over half their rims. Rolling alone would fail at one point in every turn, so a few teeth carry the motion past it. The second wheel speeds up and slows down as it goes.

How it works

The left gear turns steadily. Along the smooth halves the two rims simply roll on each other without slipping. Along the other halves the teeth take over. Watch the Touching readout change between rims and teeth.

Each gear turns on a point near one end, so the distance from shaft to rim keeps changing. Where one gear is long, the other is short by the same amount, so they always touch. A smooth rim can only push while the touching point moves out along it. When the long end of the left gear meets the short end of the right one, the push dies. Without teeth the right gear would stop there.

The tongue and the fork make sure the first teeth meet tooth to gap and not tooth to tooth. Slow the speed right down and watch the Driven readout. It is fastest when the gears touch near the right gear's shaft.

In Brown's words

Represents a mode of obtaining motion from rolling contact. The teeth are for making the motion continuous, or it would cease at the point of contact shown in the figure. The forked catch is to guide the teeth into proper contact.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Each gear is a true ellipse turning on a point near one end, called a focus. That is the shape that lets two equal ovals roll together on fixed shafts. The plate and base that hold the shafts, the sizes, the number of teeth and the shapes of the tongue and fork were made up. The fork's slot was cut to the tongue's exact path, so the two never rub.
