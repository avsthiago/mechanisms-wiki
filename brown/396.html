No. 396: Reed's anchor and lever escapement

A balance wheel swings to and fro on a coiled spring and keeps the time. A lever holds the escape wheel still between swings. The balance gets a push on every swing: one way through the lever, the other way straight from a tooth of the wheel.

How it works

The escape wheel is always trying to turn, but one pallet of the lever sits in the way of a tooth and holds it. For most of each swing the balance touches nothing at all.

When the balance swings anticlockwise, the red pin on its roller runs into the notch of the fork and knocks the lever over. The upper pallet slides off its tooth and the wheel is free. A tooth next to the roller catches the finger and pushes the balance round. Then the lower pallet stops the wheel.

On the swing back the pin knocks the lever the other way. The end of the lower pallet is sloped. As the tooth slides off it, the tooth pushes the lever, and the fork pushes the pin. So the balance is pushed on both swings, and the wheel is let go only once for each push. Try Soft, then Stiff, and count the beats.

In Brown's words

G. P. Reed's patent anchor and lever escapement for watches. The lever is so applied in combination with chronometer escapement that the whole impulse given balance in one direction is transmitted through lever, and whole impulse in opposite direction is transmitted directly to chronometer impulse pallet, locking and unlocking the escape-wheel but once at each impulse given by said wheel.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The coiled spring, the back plate, the number of teeth and the sizes were made up. The balance sits closer to the escape wheel than in the drawing, and the finger on its roller is longer and straight, so that a tooth can reach it. The two pins that stop the lever are in the drawing. The mainspring is left out and its push on the wheel is taken to be steady. The parts knock and slide on each other without rubbing, and only the balance and the wheel are slowed a little.
