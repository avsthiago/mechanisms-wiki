No. 486: Vertical windmill

A windmill that turns flat, like a roundabout, on an upright shaft. Its sails swing on pins, so they catch the wind on one side and slip back through it edge first on the other.

How it works

The wind blows up the picture. On the right, the sails are moving the same way as the wind. They stand square across it, so it pushes them along like a hand pushing a door.

On the left, the sails are coming back against the wind. Here each sail is turned edge first, so it slips through the air with hardly any push against it. The push on the right is much bigger than the push on the left, so the mill keeps turning.

Try fixed sails. Now they cannot swing. The sails on the left get pushed back just as hard as the ones on the right are pushed forward, and the mill stops.

In Brown's words

Plan of a vertical wind-mill. The sails are so pivoted as to present their edges in returning toward the wind, but to present their faces to the action of the wind, the direction of which is supposed to be as indicated by the arrow.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The lower arms, the base and the sizes were made up. Here a rule sets each sail's angle exactly. A real mill would use stops on the arms and let the wind swing the sails.
