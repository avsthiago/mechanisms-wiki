No. 146: Crank pin in a grooved yoke

A disc turns steadily and its pin runs in a groove cut in a yoke. The yoke slides up and down on a rod. If the groove is curved the right way, the yoke moves at the same speed all the way up and all the way down.

How it works

The disc turns at a steady speed and the red pin goes round in a circle. The pin sits in a groove in the yoke. The yoke can only move up and down, so the pin pushes it that way.

With a straight groove, the yoke speeds up and slows down as the pin goes round. It is fastest in the middle of each stroke and stops for a moment at the top and the bottom. With the shaped groove, the pin is steered up and down the curve. The yoke then moves at nearly the same speed for the whole stroke. It turns round quickly at each end.

Watch the speed reading while the disc turns. Try Straight, then Shaped, and compare how much the speed changes.

In Brown's words

Continuous rotary motion of the disk produces reciprocating rectilinear motion of the yoke-bar, by means of the wrist or crank-pin on the disk working in the groove of the yoke. The groove may be so shaped as to obtain a uniform reciprocating rectilinear motion.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not show the shape of the groove. It was worked out here so that the yoke moves almost evenly, which makes a curve like a figure of eight lying on its side. The groove is cut into the yoke face, with a dark floor. The guides, the plate behind the shaft, the base and all the sizes were made up. The disc is drawn in front, as in the drawing, but made see-through so the groove shows behind it. The yoke has a slot in the middle for the shaft.
