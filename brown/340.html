No. 340: Parallel motion with a rocking pillar

This beam has no fixed pivot. It sits on top of a pillar that rocks to and fro on a pin at its foot. A short radius bar holds the beam, and the end of the beam goes up and down almost straight, so the piston rod can hang straight from it.

How it works

The beam has no fixed pivot. Its middle rests on a pillar that can rock on a pin at the floor. When the beam tips, the radius bar pulls on it, and the pillar leans a little to let the beam's middle move sideways.

If the beam had a fixed pivot, its end would swing on a curve and pull the piston rod sideways. Here, as the end goes up or down, the radius bar and the pillar shift the whole beam sideways by just the right amount. So the piston end goes up and down almost straight, even when the beam tips a long way.

Watch the top of the pillar move to the side as the beam tips. Then look at the Off line reading. Try Small, then Large, and see how little it changes. Choose Closed to see the cylinder as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Another parallel motion. Beam, D, C, with joggling pillar-support, B, F, which vibrates from the center, F. The piston-rod is connected at C. The radius-bar, E, A, produces the parallel motion.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The cylinder, the wall, the bearings, the floor and all sizes were made up. The beam is rocked to and fro as if by the crank, which is left out, so the rod on the left end just hangs. The radius bar is pinned a little closer to the pillar than drawn, which makes the line straighter.
