No. 47: Friction clutch

Two shafts meet end to end. A cone on one can be pushed into a cup on the other, and rubbing makes the second shaft turn. A heavy machine then starts gently instead of with a sudden jerk.

How it works

The driving shaft and its cup turn all the time. The driven shaft only turns when the cone is pressed into the cup. Then the sloping side of the cone and the sloping wall of the cup rub hard against each other.

Rubbing is not a firm grip. At first the cup slides past the cone, and the driven shaft speeds up little by little. When both turn at the same speed they stop sliding and turn as one. Pull the cone out and the driven shaft slowly runs down.

Try Out and wait for the driven shaft to stop. Then try In. Watch its speed climb in the readout instead of jumping. Choose Closed to see the cup whole, as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

A frictional clutch-box, thrown in and out of gear by the lever at the bottom. This is used for connecting and disconnecting heavy machinery. The eye of the disk to the right has a slot which slides upon a long key or feather fixed on the shaft.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which shows the clutch cut in half. Here only a quarter of the cup is cut away, so you can see the cone inside it. The sizes, the slope of the cone, the stands and the shape of the lever were made up. Brown does not show where the driven shaft ends; here it turns in a hole in the cup's hub. The speed-up is a simple model: the rubbing adds the same amount of speed every second until the two shafts match.
