No. 316: Mercury pendulum

A clock pendulum whose weight is a glass jar of mercury. Heat makes the rod longer, which would slow the clock. The mercury swells upwards by just the right amount to cancel it.

How it works

Change the temperature and watch the jar. When it is warm, the rod gets longer and the jar drops. At the same time the mercury climbs up inside the jar. The red line stays level with the dark line on the board.

A pendulum keeps time by its length, measured down to the middle of its weight. A longer pendulum swings more slowly. With a solid weight, a warm day would make the clock lose about 7 seconds. Here the mercury rises twice as far as the rod drops, so the middle of the mercury stays at the same height.

The real changes are far too small to see, less than half a millimetre. The model makes them much bigger. Try 5 °C, then 35 °C, and keep an eye on the red line.

In Brown's words

Mercurial compensation pendulum. A glass jar of mercury is used for the bob or weight. As the pendulum-rod is expanded lengthwise by increased temperature, the expansion of mercury in jar carries it to a greater height therein, and so raises its center of gravity relatively to the rod sufficiently to compensate for downward expansion of the rod. As rod is contracted by a reduction of temperature, contraction of mercury lowers it relatively to rod. In this way the center of oscillation is always kept in the same place, and the effective length of pendulum always the same.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which shows only the jar end of the pendulum. The rod above, its spring, the bracket and the back board were made up, and the rod is drawn much shorter than a real one. The changes in length are made far larger than real ones so that you can see them. The readouts are worked out for a steel rod about a metre long, and the glass is taken not to change.
