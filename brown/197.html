No. 197: Mangle rack

A small gear turns steadily one way, yet the big square frame slides to the right and then back to the left. The gear runs along the top of a row of pins, round the end pin and back along the bottom. Mangles, machines that press wet washing, used this to push a heavy box of rollers back and forth.

How it works

An engine turns the small gear, called a pinion, always the same way. Its teeth catch the pins along the top of the row. Along the top it pushes the frame one way.

At the end of the row the pinion holds on to the last pin and swings round it. The curved guide keeps it from slipping off. Its shaft slides down a narrow slot to the underside. Along the bottom the same turning pushes the frame the other way. The pinion never changes direction, but the frame goes out and comes back.

Try Clockwise, then Anticlockwise. See which way the frame moves while the pinion is on top of the pins.

In Brown's words

What is called a "mangle-rack." A continuous rotation of the pinion will give a reciprocating motion to the square frame. The pinion-shaft must be free to rise and fall, to pass round the guides at the ends of the rack. This motion may be modified as follows:—If the square frame be fixed, and the pinion be fixed upon a shaft made with a universal joint, the end of the shaft will describe a line, similar to that shown in the drawing, around the rack.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The slotted bar that holds the shaft, the rollers, the board behind and all the sizes and numbers of teeth were made up. Brown also says the frame could stand still while a shaft with a universal joint carries the pinion round the pins. That version is not shown.
