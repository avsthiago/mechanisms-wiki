No. 28: Brush wheels

A flat disc turns on an upright shaft. A small wheel rests on its face and is turned by rubbing. Slide the small wheel nearer the edge and it spins faster, without stopping the machine.

How it works

The disc turns and the small wheel turns with it, because the two rub together. Nothing else joins them. Rubber on the disc's face helps it grip.

A point near the disc's edge travels a long way in each turn. A point near the centre hardly moves. The small wheel's rim must keep up with the part of the disc it sits on. So near the edge it spins fast, and near the centre it barely turns.

Try moving the small wheel from near the centre to near the edge while it runs. Then slide it past the centre to the other side and watch it turn the other way.

In Brown's words

These are sometimes called "brush-wheels." The relative speeds can be varied by changing the distance of the upper wheel from the center of the lower one. The one drives the other by the friction or adhesion, and this may be increased by facing the lower one with india-rubber.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows only the disc, its shaft and the small wheel. The uprights, the footstep bearing, the base and the key on the shaft were made up, as were all the sizes. A real friction drive slips a little, so the small wheel turns a bit slower than shown.
