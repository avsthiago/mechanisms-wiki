No. 88: Cam and stops

A cam turns all the time, but the disc behind it moves in jumps. Each turn of the cam pushes the disc half a turn, then lets it rest. This changes steady turning into stop-and-go turning.

How it works

The cam turns steadily. Its edge has a step. When the step comes round, it catches a stop on the disc and pushes it. While it pushes, the disc turns with the cam.

The disc turns on a centre a little to one side of the cam's shaft. So the stop being pushed slowly moves away from the cam's shaft. After half a turn it is past the top of the step and slips off. The disc rests while the cam finishes its turn. The rest of the cam's edge slopes inward, so it slides past the waiting stops. Then the step reaches the other stop, which now waits where the first one started.

Try On the cam's shaft. Now the stop always stays the same distance from the cam's shaft. It never slips off the step, so the disc just turns with the cam. Compare the two readouts each way.

In Brown's words

Continuous rotary converted into intermittent rotary motion. The disk-wheel, B, carrying the stops, C, D, turns on a center eccentric to the cam, A. On continuous rotary motion being given to the cam, A, intermittent rotary motion is imparted to the wheel, B. The stops free themselves from the offset of the cam at every half-revolution, the wheel, B, remaining at rest until the cam has completed its revolution, when the same motion is repeated.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The shape of the cam's edge was worked out so the step catches each stop and lets it go after half a turn. The plate behind, the fixed round boss the disc turns on, and all the sizes were made up. Brown does not say what keeps the disc still while it rests, so a brake pad pressed by a spring was added. The choice of disc centre was added to show why the two centres must be apart.
