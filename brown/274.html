No. 274: Governor with curved guide arms

Two balls spin round with an upright spindle. Small wheels carry them up and out along two curved arms as the speed rises. The curve is shaped so the balls balance at almost one speed, so the governor holds the engine very close to that speed.

How it works

The engine turns the spindle, and the balls spin round with it. Spinning things try to fly outwards. Here each ball can only move by rolling its wheel along a curved arm. To go further out, it must also climb.

The arms are curved like a bowl, called a parabola. It gets steeper further out. That steepness grows just as fast as the outward pull does. So at one speed the balls balance anywhere on the curve. A little faster, and they climb all the way up. A little slower, and they roll all the way down. The rods pull the sleeve up with them, and the lever closes the steam valve.

The model opens at the speed where the balls rest. Move the speed slider up by one step and watch them creep out. Then one more step. Then go back down a few steps. A tiny change in speed moves the sleeve its whole way.

In Brown's words

An engine-governor. The rise and fall of the balls, K, are guided by the parabolic curved arms, B, on which the anti-friction wheels, L, run. The rods, F, connecting the wheels, L, with the sleeve move it up and down the spindle, C, D.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The floor, the bearing, the ring under the sleeve, the lever and its post were made up. The weights of the balls and the sleeve, the curve and the rub that slows the balls were chosen so the balls rest where Brown drew them at the opening speed. Stops keep the wheels from running off the curve. The weight of the sleeve makes the balance speed rise a little as the balls go out, so it is close to one speed but not exactly.
