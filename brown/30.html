No. 30: Rectangular gears

A small square gear drives a big square gear. The small one turns steadily, but the big one keeps speeding up and slowing down. A printing press used this to turn a square roller that carried the type.

How it works

Watch the two red stripes. The stripe on the small blue gear sweeps round steadily. The stripe on the big orange gear speeds up and slows down as its sides and corners come past.

A corner of a square gear is further from the shaft than the middle of a side. So the teeth at a corner move faster than the teeth on a side, and the push on the orange gear keeps changing. A corner also needs more room than a side. That is why the orange shaft sits in a green block that slides. The spring pushes the block back so the teeth always stay together.

Slow the speed right down and watch the green block move in and out. Then choose Rounder. The gears bulge out, the block hardly moves and the speed stays steadier. Go back to Square and compare.

In Brown's words

Rectangular gears. These produce a rotary motion of the driven gear at a varying speed. They were used on a printing-press, the type of which were placed on a rectangular roller.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows only the two gears. Two square gears cannot stay in mesh on shafts that never move, so the sliding block, the rails and the spring were made up to let them run. The sides are bowed out a little and the corners are rounded so the gears roll smoothly. The sizes, the number of teeth, the red stripes and the stand were made up. The printing press is left out.
