No. 328: Cartwright's parallel motion

Two equal toothed wheels turn opposite ways, each with a crank. Two rods hang from the cranks and hold the ends of a cross bar. The cross bar, and the piston rod under it, can only go straight up and down.

How it works

The two toothed wheels are locked together, so they turn at the same speed but opposite ways. Their cranks start as mirror images, one pointing left and one pointing right. So they stay mirror images all the way round.

Each crank pulls a rod, and the rods hold the two ends of the cross bar. Because everything on the left copies everything on the right, the two rods always lean by the same amount, in opposite directions. Their sideways pulls cancel out. So the middle of the cross bar can only go straight up and down, and the piston rod is never bent.

Watch the two rod readings. They are always the same number with opposite signs. Then pick the cross bar and watch it slide straight, while the crank pins go round in circles. Choose Closed to see the cylinder as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

A parallel motion invented by Dr. Cartwright in the year 1787. The toothed wheels, C, C, have equal diameters and numbers of teeth ; and the cranks, A, A, have equal radii, and are set in opposite directions, and consequently give an equal obliquity to the connecting-rods during the revolution of the wheels. The cross-head on the piston-rod being attached to the two connecting-rods, the piston-rod is caused to move in a right line.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the cylinder only as its top cover; here it is drawn see-through so the piston shows. The posts under the cross beam, the bearings, the shaft and stand of the flywheel, the base, the tooth counts and all the sizes were made up.
