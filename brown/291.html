No. 291: Chronometer escapement

This escapement was made for chronometers, the very exact clocks once used to find a ship's place at sea. A small stop on a springy strip holds the escape wheel. The balance frees it for a moment, takes one push, and then swings on its own.

How it works

The escape wheel is always trying to turn. One tooth rests against the small stop under the long spring, so the wheel cannot move. For most of its swing the balance touches nothing at all.

When the balance swings anticlockwise, the little stud near its axis catches the end of the thin spring and lifts it. The thin spring meets the hook, so the long spring rises too and the stop lets go of its tooth. The wheel jumps forward. Another tooth, waiting beside the big roller, runs into the straight side of the notch and gives the balance a push. The stop drops back in time to catch the next tooth.

On the swing back the stud only bends the thin spring down and slips past. Nothing is let go, so the wheel moves one tooth for every two beats. The balance spring sets the pace. Try Soft, then Stiff, and count the beats.

In Brown's words

Arnold's chronometer or free escapement, sometimes used in watches. A spring, A, is fixed or screwed against the plate of the watch at b. To the under side of this spring is attached a small stop, d, against which rest successively the teeth of the escape-wheel, B ; and on the top of spring is fixed a stud, i, holding a lighter and more flexible spring which passes under a hook, k, at the extremity of A, so that it is free on being depressed, but in rising would lift A. On the axis of the balance is a small stud, a, which touches the thin spring at each oscillation of balance-wheel. When the movement is in the direction shown by the arrow, the stud depresses the spring in passing, but on returning raises it and the spring, A, and stop, d, and thus allows one tooth of escape-wheel to pass, letting them fall immediately to arrest the next. At the same time, that this tooth escapes another strikes against the side of the notch, g, and restores to balance-wheel the force lost during a vibration. It will be understood that only at one point is the free movement of balance opposed during an oscillation.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The balance wheel with its coiled spring, the back plate and the sizes were made up. Both springs are shown as stiff strips on hinges, the long one at its foot and the thin one at the hook, instead of bending along their length. The mainspring is left out and its push on the wheel is taken to be steady. The parts knock and slide on each other without rubbing, and only the balance and the wheel are slowed a little.
