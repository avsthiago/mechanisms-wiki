No. 427: Rotary engine with an eccentric shaft

In this steam engine the shaft is not in the middle of the cylinder. Its hub touches the wall at the top and leaves a wide gap at the bottom. Two pistons reach across the gap, and steam pushes them round.

How it works

Look at the blue hub. Its shaft sits above the centre of the cylinder. So the hub touches the wall at the top, between the two pipes, and leaves a wide gap round the bottom.

The orange pistons are not fixed to the hub. Each one is held on a ring round a pin at the very centre of the cylinder. That keeps its tip just touching the wall all the way round. The hub carries the pistons round with it. As it turns, they slide in and out through the round yellow bars.

Steam comes in by one pipe and pushes a piston round through the wide gap to the other pipe. Dark blue dots are fresh steam and pale dots are used steam. Dots shut in between the two pistons spread out and turn paler as their room grows. Watch how far a piston sticks out of the hub. Then swap the pipes. Choose Closed to see the engine as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Another rotary engine, in which the shaft, B, works in fixed bearings eccentric to the cylinder. The pistons, A, A, are fitted to slide in and out from grooves in the hub, C, which is concentric with the shaft, but they are always radial to the cylinder, being kept so by rings (shown dotted) fitting to hubs on the cylinder-heads. The pistons slide through rolling packings, a, a, in the hub, C.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front cover is left off, and the steam is drawn as small blue balls so you can see it move. The pin at the centre belongs to that cover, so it is drawn grey and see-through. Brown draws the two pistons in one straight line. They only line up twice in each turn, so the model starts at a different moment. The sizes, the shape of the foot and the choice of pipe were made up.
