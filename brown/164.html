No. 164: Knee lever

A handle turns a bent arm, and the arm pushes on a long strut. When the arm and the strut come close to one straight line, the foot of the strut is shoved down with a very strong force. Presses and stamps use this, because a light pull on the handle gives a hard squeeze.

How it works

Lift the handle. It turns the bent lever on its pin under the ceiling. The lower end of the lever swings round that pin, and the long strut hangs from it. The foot of the strut sits in a block that can only slide up and down.

The strut cannot get longer or shorter. As the lever swings, the lower end moves round until it is straight under the pin. Then the lever and the strut lie in one line, and the block is pushed to its lowest place. It moves only a little near the end, but the push is very strong.

Watch the knee reading and the platen reading together. When the knee gets close to a straight line, the platen hardly moves at all, although the handle still moves a lot. That is where the big force comes from.

In Brown's words

A knee-lever, differing slightly from the toggle-joint shown in 40. It is often used for presses and stamps, as a great force can be obtained by it. The action is by raising or lowering the horizontal lever.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The ceiling plate, the holder for the top pin, the block on the ground, the guide posts and all the sizes were made up. Brown's strut rests straight on the block. Here its foot is in a small platen between two posts, so it has to go straight down. The handle swings up and down so the motion repeats, and it stops just before the knee is quite straight.
