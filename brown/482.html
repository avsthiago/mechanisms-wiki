No. 482: Gas regulator

The pressure in a town's gas main goes up and down, and so does the number of lamps people light. This regulator keeps the gas going to the burners at an even pressure anyway. A floating bell senses the pressure and works a small valve.

How it works

Gas from the main comes up the pipe on the right and out through the notches of the small valve. It fills the space under the big bell, and from there it goes out of the pipe on the left to the burners. The gas under the bell pushes up on its roof.

If the pressure rises, the bell is pushed up. The lever then pushes the valve down, its notches sink into the mercury, and less gas gets through. If the pressure falls, the bell sinks and the valve opens wider. The bell settles where the gas just holds up its weight, so the pressure to the burners stays nearly the same.

The bell is far bigger than the valve, so the push on the bell wins. Watch the main pressure swing while the burner pressure hardly changes. Then light 2 burners, then 12, and see how far the valve opens. Choose Closed to see the regulator as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Gas regulator (Powers's patent) for equalizing the supply of gas to all the burners of a building or apartment, notwithstanding variations in the pressure on the main, or variations produced by turning gas on or off, to or from any number of the burners. The regulator-valve, D, of which a separate outside view is given, is arranged over inlet-pipe, E, and connected by a lever, d, with an inverted cup, H, the lower edges of which, as well as those of valve, dip into channels containing quicksilver. There is no escape of gas around the cup, H, but there are notches, b, in the valve to permit the gas to pass over the surface of the quicksilver. As the pressure of gas increases, it acts upon the inner surface of cup, H, which is larger than valve, and the cup is thereby raised, causing a depression of the valve into the quicksilver, and contracting the opening notches, b, and diminishing the quantity of gas passing through. As the pressure diminishes, an opposite result is produced. The outlet to burners is at F.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The box is round and cut in half so you can see inside. The post for the lever, the sizes, the weight of the bell and the pressures are made up, and the outside view of the valve is left out. The mercury is drawn level; in a real regulator the gas pushes it a little lower inside the bell and the valve.
