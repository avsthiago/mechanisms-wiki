No. 118: Doubling the stroke

A small gear rides between two toothed bars, called racks. The lower rack is fixed and the upper one can slide. When the gear is pushed along, the upper rack moves twice as far as the gear's centre. A short push gives a long stroke.

How it works

The crank pushes the rod, and the rod carries the gear to and fro. The gear rolls on the fixed lower rack, so it turns as it goes. Its top teeth push the upper rack along.

Think of the gear's centre moving a certain way. The top of a rolling gear moves twice as fast as its centre. So the upper rack goes twice as far as the gear's centre, in the same direction. Check the two readings: one is always double the other.

Try Short, then Long. The gear's centre travels farther each time, and the top rack always travels twice that.

In Brown's words

A mode of doubling the length of stroke of a piston-rod, or the throw of a crank. A pinion revolving on a spindle attached to the connecting-rod or pitman is in gear with a fixed rack. Another rack carried by a guide-rod above, and in gear with the opposite side of the pinion, is free to traverse backward and forward. Now, as the connecting-rod communicates to the pinion the full length of stroke, it would cause the top rack to traverse the same distance, if the bottom rack was alike movable; but as the latter is fixed, the pinion is made to rotate, and consequently the top rack travels double the distance.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows only the rod, the gear and the two racks. The crank, the upright plate, the guide strip above the sliding rack, the base under the fixed rack and all the sizes and tooth counts were made up. The choice of crank arm was added to show the doubling at different strokes.
