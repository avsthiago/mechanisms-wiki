No. 109: Screw-cutting by a guide screw

A long guide screw pulls a small carriage steadily down the frame. The carriage holds a cutter that scratches a thread into a rod turning beside it. The gears at the bottom decide how close together the new threads are.

How it works

The guide screw turns, and a tooth on the carriage sits in its thread. The carriage cannot turn, so it is pulled along the screw. Its arm carries the cutter down beside the rod at a steady speed.

Two gears at the foot make the rod turn with the screw. While the rod turns, the cutter moves along it, so the cutter draws a spiral. The distance the cutter moves in one turn of the rod is the gap between threads. When the cutter gets to the end, it is pulled back and a new rod starts.

A big gear on the rod makes it turn slowly, so the cutter moves a long way in each turn and the threads are far apart. A small gear does the opposite. Try the three choices and compare the dark spirals.

In Brown's words

The rotation of the screw at the left-hand side produces a uniform rectilinear movement of a cutter which cuts another screw thread. The pitch of the screw to be cut may be varied by changing the sizes of the wheels at the end of the frame.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The top bar, the base, the guide rod that stops the carriage turning, the tooth, the sizes and the tooth counts of the gears were made up. Brown draws the two gears as one wide gear. Here they meet directly, so the new thread winds the opposite way to the guide thread; real machines add a third gear to fix that. The cutter is pulled back and the carriage wound up to start a new rod, which Brown does not show. The choice of gears was added to show what it changes.
