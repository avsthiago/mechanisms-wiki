No. 143: Sliding worm and traverse

A turning shaft drives a screw, called a worm, that turns a gear, called a worm wheel. The wheel and screw ride together on a small frame. A rod from the wheel to a fixed post pushes the frame to and fro. A steady turn becomes a back and forth slide.

How it works

Turn the pulley and the long shaft turns the worm. The worm slides on the shaft but cannot spin on it, because a key in its hub fits a groove in the shaft. Its thread pushes the teeth of the wheel round, one tooth for each turn.

The wheel and the worm both sit in a small frame that slides on a bar. The wheel has a pin near its rim. A rod joins that pin to the fixed post on the right. As the wheel turns, the pin swings round and the rod pushes and pulls the frame.

The worm is carried along with the frame, so it stays in step with the wheel. Try the pin near the middle, then near the rim, and compare how far the frame slides.

In Brown's words

Circular motion into alternate rectilinear motion. Motion is transmitted through pulley at the left upon the worm-shaft. Worm slides upon shaft, but is made to turn with it by means of a groove cut in shaft, and a key in hub of worm. Worm is carried by a small traversing-frame, which slides upon a horizontal bar of the fixed frame, and the traversing-frame also carries the toothed wheel into which the worm gears. One end of a connecting-rod is attached to fixed frame at the right and the other end to a wrist secured in toothed wheel. On turning worm-shaft, rotary motion is transmitted by worm to wheel, which, as it revolves, is forced by connecting-rod to make an alternating traverse motion.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The posts, the base, the bar, the sizes, the number of teeth and the places of the pin were made up. The key and groove are not drawn; the worm simply turns with the shaft. The wheel has plain straight teeth, as drawn. A real worm wheel has slanted, hollowed teeth.
