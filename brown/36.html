No. 36: Mangle wheel and pinion

A small gear turns steadily one way, yet the big wheel turns one way and then back again. The small gear runs along both sides of a curved strip of teeth. Mangles, machines that press wet washing between rollers, used this to roll the cloth back and forth.

How it works

The small gear, called a pinion, is turned at a steady speed. It runs along the outside of the red strip of teeth and pushes the wheel round. At the end of the strip it rolls round the end and comes back along the inside.

On the inside the same turning pushes the wheel the other way, so the wheel goes back. A minus sign in the readout means the other way. The inside edge is a smaller circle than the outside edge, so the wheel goes back a little faster. The pinion's shaft slides up and down in the slot of the green bar to change sides. A groove in the wheel holds the end of the shaft, so the teeth cannot come apart.

Try Long, then Short. Watch how far the wheel turns before it goes back.

In Brown's words

Mangle-wheel and pinion—so called from their application to mangles—converts continuous rotary motion of pinion into reciprocating rotary motion of wheel. The shaft of pinion has a vibratory motion, and works in a straight slot cut in the upright stationary bar to allow the pinion to rise and fall and work inside and outside of the gearing of the wheel. The slot cut in the face of the mangle-wheel and following its outline is to receive and guide the pinion-shaft and keep the pinion in gear.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The post behind the wheel, the base, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. Brown draws the groove further from the teeth near the middle of the wheel. Here it keeps the same distance from the teeth all the way round, so the pinion stays in mesh. The short strip was added to show what the length changes.
