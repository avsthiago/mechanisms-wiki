No. 280: Lever-worked windlass

A way to turn a heavy windlass by rocking a long hand lever. A short lever grips the rim of the wheel, pushes it round a little, then lets go and slides back for the next push. Nothing has teeth except the ratchet that stops the wheel running back.

How it works

Rock the hand lever and the rod goes up and down. On the way up, the short lever first turns on its pin until its round end meets the rim. Then the rim is caught tight between that end and the ledges on the inside. The block, the short lever and the wheel all turn together.

On the way down, the short lever turns back and its end lifts off the rim. Now nothing grips, so the block slides back down the rim. The wheel stays put, because the holding pawls behind it drop into the ratchet teeth. The harder you lift, the harder the rim is squeezed, so it does not slip.

The first part of every lift is spent closing the small gap at the round end. Only the rest of the lift turns the wheel. Try a short swing and see how little the wheel moves. Then try a long one.

In Brown's words

A mode of working a windlass. By the alternating motion of the long hand-lever to the right, motion is communicated to the short lever, the end of which is in immediate contact with the rim of the wheel. The short lever has a very limited motion upon a pin, which is fixed in a block of cast-iron, which is made with two jaws, each having a flange projecting inward in contact with the inner surface of the rim of the wheel. By the upward motion of the outward end of the short lever, the rim of the wheel is jammed between the end of the lever and the flanges of the block, so as to cause friction sufficient to turn the wheel by the further upward movement of the lever. The backward movement of the wheel is prevented by a common ratchet-wheel and pawls ; as the short lever is pushed down it frees the wheel and slides freely over it.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown mentions the ratchet and holding pawls but does not show the ratchet; here it sits behind the wheel, and the two rods drawn from the middle post are read as the holding pawls. The barrel and rope of the windlass are left out, as in the drawing. The bearing for the wheel's shaft, the studs, the sizes and the amount of play in the short lever were made up.
