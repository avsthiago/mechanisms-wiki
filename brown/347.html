No. 347: Disc engine

A steam engine whose piston is a flat disc instead of a plug in a tube. The disc wobbles like a spinning coin settling on a table, and a rod through its middle turns a crank. There is no back-and-forth stroke, so it can run smoothly and fast.

How it works

The disc always leans over. One edge rests against the left cone and the opposite edge against the right cone. These two lines of touching cut the inside into spaces, and steam goes into them by turns.

The steam pushes the disc where it is not touching, so the touching line rolls round the cones. The disc rocks like a coin settling on a table. The rod sticks straight out of the disc, so its end swings round a circle and turns the flywheel once for every roll of the disc.

Turn the model and look along the shaft. The rod's end goes round, but the disc itself never turns round: watch it only rock. Try a slow speed to follow the line where it touches each cone. Choose Closed to see the case as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Section of disk engine. Disk piston, seen edgewise, has a motion substantially like a coin when it first falls after being spun in the air. The cylinder-heads are cones. The piston-rod is made with a ball to which the disk is attached, said ball working in concentric seats in cylinder-heads, and the left-hand end is attached to the crank-arm or fly-wheel on end of shaft at left. Steam is admitted alternately on either side of piston.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The casing is cut away like Brown's section, but the disc, ball and rod are shown whole. The steam pipes and valves, and the fixed plate that stops the disc from spinning, are left out. Brown also draws a curved bar over the top tying both ends of the rod together; it is left out, and the rod ends inside the right cone. The base, the bearing, the socket on the flywheel and all sizes were made up.
