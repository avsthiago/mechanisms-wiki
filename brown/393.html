No. 393: Lens polishing

A cup of polishing powder rides on a glass dome. A turning shaft drives the cup round the dome, and the cup also turns on itself. Every part of the cup meets a different part of the glass, so the dome wears evenly.

How it works

The shaft turns and carries the arm round with it. The arm holds the cup a little to one side of the shaft, so the cup does not sit still on top of the dome. It circles round the top.

Because the cup is held on a ball joint, it does not just go round. It also turns on its own axis. The red dot on the cup moves round the cup every time the shaft turns once. The red line on the glass shows that the cup never goes over exactly the same path twice, because the pattern slowly turns round.

That is the point: the same spot on the cup does not keep rubbing the same spot on the glass. Try Small, then Big. The bigger the circle, the faster the pattern turns round.

In Brown's words

Contrivance for polishing lenses and bodies of spherical form. The polishing material is in a cup connected by a ball-and-socket joint and bent piece of metal with a rotating upright shaft set concentric to the body to be polished. The cup is set eccentric, and by that means is caused to have an independent rotary motion about its axis on the universal joint, as well as to revolve about the common axis of the shaft and the body to be polished. This prevents the parts of the surface of the cup from coming repeatedly in contact with the same parts of surface of the lens or other body

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The table legs, the post and the beam that hold the shaft are made up so it stands. The cup is turned on its ball joint by the glass, with no twist about its own axis. The red line is traced from one point on the edge of the cup.
