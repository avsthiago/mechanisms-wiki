No. 355: Gyroscope

A heavy disc spins inside a ring. One side of the ring rests on top of a post, and nothing holds up the other side. While the disc spins fast, the ring does not drop. It moves slowly round the post instead.

How it works

Look at the ring. It is held up at one side only, where its pin sits on the post. You would expect the other side to fall. While the disc spins fast it stays out level and creeps round the post.

The weight still pulls the ring down. But a spinning disc does not tip the way it is pushed. It turns sideways to the push. So the pull of the weight sends the ring round the post and not down. A fast disc needs only a slow drift to hold itself up.

Try Slow. The disc has less spin, so the ring nods, sags and has to go round the post faster. Then try Stopped. Now nothing holds it up, and it drops and hangs against the post.

In Brown's words

The gyroscope or rotascope, an instrument illustrating the tendency of rotating bodies to preserve their plane of rotation. The spindle of the metallic disk, C, is fitted to turn easily in bearings in the ring, A. If the disk is set in rapid rotary motion on its axis, and the pintle, F, at one side of the ring, A, is placed on the bearing in the top of the pillar, G, the disk and ring seem indifferent to gravity, and instead of dropping begin to revolve about the vertical axis.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The post is drawn taller than Brown's so the ring has room to hang, and the block under the foot was added for the same reason. The sizes and the two speeds were made up. Each time you choose a speed, the ring is let go from level again. The motion is worked out from the weight and spin of the disc alone. The weight of the ring is left out. A little rubbing at the pin makes the nodding die away. The disc never slows down here, though a real one would. The disc is drawn turning 60 times slower than it really does.
