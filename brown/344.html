No. 344: Oscillating engine

Steam pushes a piston to and fro inside a cylinder, and a crank turns that push into a spin. Here the cylinder is held at its middle on two pegs, so it can rock. The piston rod can then join the crank directly, with no sliding guide.

How it works

Watch the cylinder. It does not stay still. It rocks a little to one side, then the other, once for every turn of the crank. The pegs at its middle are the pivot.

The rod is part of the piston, and it is joined to the crank pin. The pin goes round in a circle, so the rod always points at it. The cylinder has to turn to keep up. Because it turns with the rod, nothing pushes the piston sideways, and no guide is needed.

Try a short crank arm, then a long one. The long arm makes the piston travel further, and the cylinder rocks more. Choose Closed to see the cylinder as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Oscillating engine. The cylinder has trunnions at the middle of its length working in fixed bearings, and the piston-rod is connected directly with the crank, and no guides are used.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front half of the cylinder is cut away so the piston shows. The two fixed beams, the pegs, the shaft and all the sizes were made up. Steam pipes and valves are left out.
