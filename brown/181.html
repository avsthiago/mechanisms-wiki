No. 181: Diagonal catch hand-gear

Two handles work the steam valves of a big pumping engine. The piston rod knocks them over by itself at each end of its stroke. Two hooked catches lock each handle in place until the other one is knocked over.

How it works

The piston rod moves up and down beside two handles. It carries two red blocks called tappets. Each handle turns a shaft, and a crank on that shaft raises or lowers a valve rod. The valves let steam in below or above the piston.

Here the piston is rising, with steam below it. Near the top, the lower tappet lifts the lower handle. That shuts the steam below. As the handle turns, its catch slides out from under the pin of the upper catch. The upper handle is free now, so its back weight swings it up and lets steam in above. The piston turns round and goes down. The pin on the lower catch now rests on the upper catch, and that holds the lower handle up.

Near the bottom it all happens the other way round. The upper tappet pushes the upper handle down, and the lower handle drops back. Set the speed to the lowest and watch the two pins on the catches trade places.

In Brown's words

Diagonal catch or hand-gear used in large blowing and pumping engines. In 181 the lower steam-valve and upper eduction-valve are open, while the upper steam-valve and lower eduction-valve are shut; consequently the piston will be ascending. In the ascent of the piston-rod the lower handle will be struck by the projecting tappet, and, being raised, will become engaged by the catch and shut the upper eduction and lower steam valves; at the same time, the upper handle being disengaged from the catch, the back weight will pull the handle up and open the upper steam and lower eduction valves, when the piston will consequently descend. 182 represents the position of the catchers and handles when the piston is at the top of the cylinder. In going down, the tappet of the piston-rod strikes the upper handle and throws the catches and handles to the position shown in 181.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The wall, the guides, the back weights and where they hang are made up. Brown speaks of one tappet. Here the rod has two, one for each handle, so it never has to pass a handle on the way. The catches are drawn as hooked arms that lock each other with small pins. The middle pin in the drawing is left out. The valves are not shown, and the piston is moved by steam in a simple way, with no real engine behind it.
