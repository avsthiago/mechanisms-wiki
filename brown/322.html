No. 322: Triangle parallel ruler

Two triangles are cut from one rectangle and laid with their slanting edges together. One slides along the other, and its bottom edge always points the same way. A pencil along that edge draws lines that never meet.

How it works

Look at the long slanting edges. They lie face to face, so the sliding triangle can only move along that line. It cannot turn while it slides.

Because it only slides, every edge keeps its direction. The pencil runs along the bottom edge each time, so all the lines point the same way and never meet. The farther the triangle slides, the farther apart the lines are.

Try Close spacing, then Wide. A longer slide gives wider gaps, but the lines stay parallel.

In Brown's words

A very convenient construction of parallel ruler for drawing, made by cutting a quadrangle through the diagonal, forming two right-angled triangles, A and B. It is used by sliding the hypothenuse of one triangle upon that of the other.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The sheet of paper and the pencil were added to show the lines. The two triangles are the same thickness, each with a finger hole as drawn. In real use you press one triangle down with a hand and push the other; here the upper one stays still. The sizes are made up.
