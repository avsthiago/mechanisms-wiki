No. 348: Two strokes per turn

A disc with two slots that cross at right angles turns on a shaft. Two pins on a long rod sit one in each slot. The rod is pushed up and down twice for every turn of the disc, which is handy where a part must work faster than the shaft turns.

How it works

The disc turns and the two slides move in and out along their slots. They are fixed to the rod by pins, so they drag it up and down. The slot arms are at right angles, so while one slide is near the middle the other is far out.

First one slot pulls the rod down, then the other slot pushes it up. Each takes its turn in a half turn of the disc. So the rod makes two full strokes in one turn. The same idea drove the needle of a sewing machine and the blades of a saw.

The slider changes the gap between the two pins. Try a narrow gap, then a wide one, and watch how far the rod travels. The number of strokes does not change.

In Brown's words

Mode of obtaining two reciprocating movements of a rod by one revolution of a shaft, patented in 1836 by B. F. Snyder, has been used for operating the needle of a sewing machine, by J. S. McCurdy, also for driving a gang of saws. The disk, A, on the central rotating shaft has two slots, a, a, crossing each other at a right angle in the center, and the connecting-rod, B, has attached to it two pivoted slides, c, c, one working in each slot.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not show what holds the rod, so a fixed pin in a long slot at the top of the rod was added to keep it upright. The rod tilts a little as it moves, because it must swing a little to let both pins stay in their slots. The shaft, the support behind it and all the sizes were made up.
