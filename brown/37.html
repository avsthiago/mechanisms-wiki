No. 37: Bevel pinion and spiral studs

A toothed cone turns at a steady speed. It drives a second cone that speeds up through each turn, then drops back to slow. The trick is a row of studs that climbs round the second cone in a spiral.

How it works

The left cone turns steadily. One of its teeth catches a stud on the right cone and pushes it along. Then the next tooth catches the next stud. The stud being pushed is shown in red. The two cones turn opposite ways, so the driven speed has a minus sign.

The studs climb round the right cone in a spiral. Low down, the toothed cone is thin and the stud wheel is wide, so the stud wheel turns slowly. Higher up, the toothed cone is wide and the stud wheel is thin, so the same push turns the stud wheel much faster. Each stud is higher than the last, so the stud wheel speeds up step by step.

After the top stud, the bottom stud comes round again and the speed drops back all at once. Try Steep climb, then Gentle climb. Watch the driven speed and see which spiral changes it more.

In Brown's words

Uniform into variable rotary motion. The bevel-wheel or pinion to the left has teeth cut through the whole width of its face. Its teeth work with a spirally arranged series of studs on a conical wheel.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not say which cone drives, how far the spiral climbs or what happens when it ends. Here the toothed cone drives and the spiral goes once round, so the stud wheel drops from its fastest to its slowest with a knock each turn. The speed changes in small steps, one for each stud. The stand, the sizes, the number of teeth and studs and the gentle spiral were made up.
