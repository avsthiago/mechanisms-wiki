No. 426: Rotary engine with sliding pistons

Steam turns a round hub inside a casing with two bulges. Two flat pistons slide in and out of the hub so they always reach the wall. Steam pushes on both pistons at once, so the shaft is pulled round evenly.

How it works

Look at the two curved rooms, one above the hub and one below. An orange piston stands across each room like a door. Steam comes in behind the piston, shown as dark blue dots, and pushes it along. The pale dots in front of it are used steam on its way out.

At the left and the right the casing closes in until it touches the hub. Steam cannot get past there. But the pistons must get past, so they slide back into the hub. Once through, they slide out again into the next room.

Each room has its own pipe in and its own pipe out. So both pistons are pushed at the same time, on opposite sides of the shaft. Watch how far a piston sticks out as it goes round. Then swap the pipes and the shaft turns the other way. Choose Closed to see the engine as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Another form of rotary engine, in which there are two stationary abutments, D, D, within the cylinder, and the two pistons, A, A, in order to enable them to pass the abutments, are made to slide radially in grooves in the hub, C, of the main shaft, B. The steam acts on both pistons at once, to produce the rotation of the hub and shaft. The induction and eduction are indicated by arrows.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front cover is left off, and the steam is drawn as small blue balls so you can see it move. Brown does not show what pushes the pistons out against the wall, so here they simply follow it. For a moment, while a piston crosses the mouth of a pipe, nothing separates the two pipes of that room. The straight pipes, the exact curve of the wall, the sizes and the choice of pipe were made up.
