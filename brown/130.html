No. 130: Plate shears

A pair of jaws cuts iron plate like giant scissors. A turning cam lifts the long arm to shut the jaws. The heavy arm then falls and opens them again, ready for the next cut.

How it works

The cam wheel turns. Its shaft is not in the middle of the wheel, so the top of the wheel rises and falls as it turns. The long arm rests on top of the wheel and goes up and down with it.

The arm and the jaws rock on one pin, like a seesaw. When the arm goes up, the blade at the other end goes down and shuts on the plate. When the wheel drops away, the heavy arm falls under its own weight. That opens the jaws. No spring is needed.

Try the three settings of the cam, and watch the jaw gap. With the cam only a little off-centre, the arm does not rise far enough, and the jaws never close.

In Brown's words

Shears for cutting iron plates, etc. The jaws are opened by the weight of the long arm of the upper one, and closed by the rotation of the cam.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The shape of the jaws, the frame, the pin and its side plates, the post that holds the cam's shaft and the size of the wheel were made up. The pin is placed on the cutting edge of the upper jaw, as in a pair of scissors. No plate is shown, so the jaws close fully. The arm falls freely under its own weight. The choice of how far the cam is off-centre was added to compare.
