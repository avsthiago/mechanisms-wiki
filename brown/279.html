No. 279: Crank and slotted cross-head

A crank pin carries a block that slides up and down in a tall slot. The slotted frame, called a cross-head, is pushed from side to side. So a turning motion becomes a straight to-and-fro motion.

How it works

The crank pin goes round in a circle. The box on the pin goes round with it. But the slot only lets the box slide up and down. So the box pushes the cross-head sideways instead.

The cross-head goes as far to each side as the pin does, no further. It moves fastest when the pin is level with the shaft. It stops for a moment when the pin is at its highest or lowest point. The box is two liners and two wedges. When they wear, the screws push the wedges further in, so the box fills the slot again.

Try a short throw, then a long one. The pin goes round a smaller circle, so the cross-head moves a shorter way. Watch the box slide a shorter way in the slot too.

In Brown's words

Crank and slotted cross-head, with Clayton's sliding journal-box applied to the crank-wrist. This box consists of two taper lining pieces and two taper gibs adjustable by screws, which serve at the same time to tighten the box on the wrist and to set it out to the slot in the cross-head as the box and wrist wear.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows only the cross-head, the box and the pin. The crank arm, its shaft and bearing, the guides, the base and all the sizes were made up. The wedges and screws are drawn but do not move. Brown's picture is a view along the shaft, and the model is built the same way.
