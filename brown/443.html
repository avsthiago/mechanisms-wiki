No. 443: Archimedes's screw driven by the stream

A long tube with a spiral inside it leans over a stream. A paddle wheel at its lower end turns in the current, and it turns the tube too. Water climbs up the spiral and pours out at the top.

How it works

The lower end of the tube is in the water. The stream pushes the paddles, so the paddle wheel and the tube turn together. Water fills the lowest part of the tube near the water.

The spiral blade turns round with the tube. Each pocket of water sits at the bottom of the tube, and the turning blade moves it along the slope. Watch the blue pockets climb, one after another, up to the top end. There they pour out into the tank.

Try Slow, then Fast. Count how many pockets are poured out in the same time. Choose Closed to see the tube as it really is, not see-through.

In Brown's words

Application of Archimedes's screw to raising water, the supply stream being the motive power. The oblique shaft of the wheel has extending through it a spiral passage, the lower end of which is immersed in water, and the stream, acting upon the wheel at its lower end, produces its revolution, by which the water is conveyed upward continuously through the spiral passage and discharged at the top.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The two bearings and their posts, the tank, the stream bed and the water are made up. Brown's drawing shows a handle over the top end; it is left out. The number of turns in the spiral, the number of paddles and the speeds are chosen to look right. The pockets are drawn as blobs of water, not worked out from real flow.
