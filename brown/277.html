No. 277: Revolver cylinder ratchet

Pulling back the hammer of this revolver also turns its cylinder. A small finger on the hammer, called a pawl, pushes a ratchet on the back of the cylinder. Each time the hammer is pulled back, the next chamber turns up to the top, ready to fire.

How it works

Pull the hammer back and it swings on its pin. Low down on the hammer is a pin that carries a thin finger. As the hammer swings back, that pin moves up, and the finger rises with it.

A spring presses the tip of the finger against the saw teeth on the back of the cylinder. Going up, the tip meets the steep side of a tooth and pushes it, so the cylinder turns one sixth of a turn. When the hammer falls, the finger slides back down over the sloping side of the next tooth and drops in behind it, ready for the next shot.

Pause, then move the hammer slider slowly from down to all the way back. Watch the finger lift the tooth. Then move it back to down and watch the finger slip over a tooth while the cylinder stays still.

In Brown's words

Col. Colt's invention for obtaining the movement of the cylinder of a revolving fire-arm by the act of cocking the hammer. As the hammer is drawn back to cock it, the dog, a, attached to the tumbler, acts on the ratchet, b, on the back of the cylinder. The dog is held up to the ratchet by a spring, c.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The frame plate, the base, the block that holds the spring and the front bearing of the cylinder were made up. The hammer outline is traced from the drawing. In a real revolver a stop locks the cylinder after each turn and a main spring throws the hammer forward; both are left out, and here the cylinder simply stays where the finger leaves it. The end of the spring is fixed to one spot on the finger here; in a real gun it slides a little along it. The trigger and the link at the bottom of the hammer are left out too.
