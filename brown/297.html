No. 297: Lantern-wheel escapement

This escape wheel has round pins standing out of its face instead of teeth. A swinging arm carries two slanted bars that take turns to catch a pin. Each swing of the pendulum lets the wheel move on by half the gap between two pins.

How it works

The weight tries to turn the wheel clockwise, so the pins on the right move downward. A pin comes down onto the top face of the upper bar and stops. The wheel cannot move.

The pin presses on the sloping bar and pushes it to the right. As the pendulum swings that way, the pin slides down the bar and slips off its lower end. It drops a short way onto the lower bar. Now the pin pushes this bar to the left, and as the pendulum swings back the pin slides along it and drops off the end. Then the next pin lands on the upper bar. These pushes keep the pendulum swinging. At the end of each swing the bar pushes the pin back a little before it lets go. The Recoil number shows how far.

The pendulum sets the pace. A long pendulum swings more slowly, so the beats come further apart. Try Short, then Long, and count the beats.

In Brown's words

An escapement with a lantern wheel. An arm, A, carries the two pallets, B and C.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown says only that an arm carries the two pallets, so how they work is worked out from the picture. His arrow shows the wheel turning the other way, but with the bars placed as drawn the pins can only keep a pendulum going when the wheel turns clockwise, so it does here. The lower end of the upper bar is cut shorter than drawn, because as drawn a pin cannot quite pass between the two bars. The pendulum, the back plate and the sizes were made up. The arm is drawn in front of the pins and see-through, where Brown shows it as a dotted outline. The weight that turns the wheel is left out and its pull is taken to be steady. The pins slide on the bars without rubbing, and only the pendulum and the wheel are slowed a little.
