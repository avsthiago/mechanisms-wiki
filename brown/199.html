No. 199: Mangle rack with a lantern pinion

A wheel with pins on one side only turns steadily one way, yet the frame round it slides to the right and then back to the left. The pins push on teeth along the top of the opening, then on teeth along the bottom. Mangles, machines that press wet washing, used this to roll a heavy box back and forth.

How it works

A shaft from behind turns a small disc with round pins standing out of it. It is called a lantern pinion, because old ones looked like a lantern. While the pins pass over the top, they catch the top teeth and push the frame one way.

The pins cover less than half of the disc. So when they reach the bottom teeth, they have already let go of the top ones. Now they push the frame the other way. For a moment, while the pins swing round the end, no pin touches any tooth and the frame stands still. The long first tooth on each row makes sure the first pin lands in the right gap.

Try Clockwise, then Anticlockwise. Watch which tooth the first pin meets each time.

In Brown's words

Another form of mangle-rack. The lantern-pinion revolves continuously in one direction, and gives reciprocating motion to the square frame, which is guided by rollers or grooves. The pinion has only teeth in less than half of its circumference, so that while it engages one side of the rack, the toothless half is directed against the other. The large tooth at the commencement of each rack is made to insure the teeth of the pinion being properly in gear.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The rollers' supports, the board behind and all the sizes were made up. The pins are held at the back only, so they and the teeth stay in sight. The pins let go of one row just before they reach the other, so the teeth have to be shallow. The shape of every tooth was worked out from the paths of the pins, so no pin ever runs into a tooth.
