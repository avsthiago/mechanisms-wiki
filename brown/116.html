No. 116: Double rack instead of a crank

A frame slides back and forth in a straight line, like a drawer. Two toothed bars on the frame turn one shaft, and the shaft always spins the same way. It turns straight pushing and pulling into steady turning, the job a crank does.

How it works

The frame slides left and right. Its two toothed bars turn two gears on the shaft. Because one bar is below the shaft and the other above it, the gears always turn opposite ways. Each gear has a finger called a pawl that rests on a ratchet wheel, a wheel with slanted teeth. The ratchet wheel is fixed to the shaft.

When the frame goes right, the front gear turns one way. Its pawl catches a tooth and pushes the shaft round. The back gear turns the other way, so its pawl slips over the teeth and clicks. When the frame goes left, the jobs swap. The back gear now pushes the shaft the same way as before. So the shaft never turns back.

Watch the pawl that turns red. After each change of direction there is a short wait before the other pawl catches a tooth. Try the Short stroke, then the Long one, and compare how far the red mark on the shaft goes round with each stroke.

In Brown's words

A substitute for the crank. Reciprocating rectilinear motion of the frame carrying the double rack produces a uniform rotary motion of the pinion-shaft. A separate pinion is used for each rack, the two racks being in different planes. Both pinions are loose on the shaft. A ratchet-wheel is fast on the shaft outside of each pinion, and a pawl attached to the pinion to engage in it, one ratchet-wheel having its teeth set in one direction and the other having its teeth set in the opposite direction. When the racks move one way, one pinion turns the shaft by means of its pawl and ratchet; and when the racks move the opposite way, the other pinion acts in the same way, one pinion always turning loosely on the shaft.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the oval frame with a rod at each end. The bed, the back plate, the red block, the dials on the shaft ends, the sizes and the numbers of teeth were made up. The gears are placed on opposite sides of the shaft so that they turn opposite ways, which is what lets both ratchet wheels turn the shaft the same way. A real shaft would coast a little between pushes. Here it stops at once.
