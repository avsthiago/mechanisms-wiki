No. 232: Lifted pawl and toothed wheel

A long handle rocks up and down. Lifting it first raises a small hook, called a pawl, out of the teeth. Lowering it drops the hook into a gap and pulls the wheel round. So rocking the handle turns the wheel in steps.

How it works

Rock the handle up and down. It is pinned to a tall plate that turns on the same shaft as the wheel. At the top of the plate a curved hook, the pawl, rests with its point between two teeth.

When you lift the handle, it first tilts a little on its pin. The rod then raises the point out of the gap. Lifting further swings the whole plate back, and the hook rides over the teeth. When you lower the handle, it first tilts back and the point drops into a gap. Lowering further drags the plate and the wheel forward.

Try a short swing. The handle tilts and the hook lifts, but the plate never swings far enough to reach the next gap, so the wheel does not move. Try the middle swing, then a wide one, and watch the wheel gain more teeth on each swing.

In Brown's words

Intermittent circular motion is imparted to the toothed wheel by vibrating the arm, B. When the arm, B, is lifted, the pawl, C, is raised from between the teeth of the wheel, and, traveling backward over the circumference, again drops between two teeth on lowering the arm, and draws with it the wheel.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The pins, the stops that limit how far the handle tilts, the board, the base, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. They were chosen so the point really lifts clear of the teeth, travels back and drops into a gap. The drawing shows two rods. Here there is one. The wheel is held still between pushes, as a little rubbing would do in a real machine.
