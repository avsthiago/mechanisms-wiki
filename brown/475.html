No. 475: Bilge ejector

A pump with no moving parts. A fast jet of steam blows up through a round chamber and drags water along with it. It was used to empty the dirty water from the bottom of a ship.

How it works

Steam rushes out of the narrow end of the bent pipe and shoots straight up the discharge pipe. At first the chamber is full of air. Watch the water climb up the suction pipe until it fills the chamber.

A fast jet drags the air or water next to it along with it. So the jet carries the air out of the top, and the air pressing on the water outside pushes water up the suction pipe to take its place. Then the jet drags the water out, and more water follows in a steady stream. The steam meets the cold water and turns back into water, so it vanishes into the stream.

Try squeezed air instead of steam. It lifts the water just the same, but air does not turn into water. Watch its bubbles ride up the discharge pipe. Choose Closed to see the pipes as they look from outside.

In Brown's words

Bilge ejector (Brear's patent) for discharging bilgewater from vessels, or for raising and forcing water under various circumstances. D is a chamber having attached a suction-pipe, B, and discharge-pipe, C, and having a steam-pipe entering at one side, with a nozzle directed toward the discharge-pipe. A jet of steam entering through A expels the air from D and C, produces a vacuum in B, and causes water to rise through B, and pass through D and C, in a regular and constant stream. Compressed air may be used as a substitute for steam.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front half of the pipes and chamber is cut away so the inside shows, as in the engraving. The length of the pipes, the speeds and the flow numbers are made up. The dots show the water, steam and air. Real water near the jet swirls and mixes; here it simply flows up along smooth paths.
