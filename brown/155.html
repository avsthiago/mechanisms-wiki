No. 155: Pawl on a bell crank

A rod moves up and down, and a ratchet wheel turns in small steps. A lever bent at a corner, called a bell crank, rocks to and fro and carries a pawl that pushes the teeth round. Set the pawl on the other side and the wheel turns the other way.

How it works

The crank at the top moves the rod up and down. The rod pulls the end of the bell crank, so the lever rocks a short way one side and then back. The pawl hangs from the other arm of the lever and rocks with it.

On one swing the pawl catches the side of a tooth and pushes the wheel round. On the other swing it slides up the other side of the tooth and drops into the next gap. The wheel stands still while that happens, so it moves in steps.

To turn the wheel the other way, set the pawl on the other side. It then catches the other side of the teeth. Try Right side, then Left side, and watch the red mark. Planing machines use this to feed the work a little at a time.

In Brown's words

Reciprocating rectilinear motion into intermittent circular motion by means of the pawl attached to the elbow-lever, and operating in the toothed wheel. Motion is given to the wheel in either direction according to the side on which the pawl works. This is used in giving the feed-motion to planing machines and other tools.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the rod cut off at the top, so a small crank was added to move it. The plate, the base, the pins, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. The pawl is a straight arm. When the pawl is moved to the other side, the machine waits.
