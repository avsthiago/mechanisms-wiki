No. 33: Elliptical gears

Two oval gears turn each other. The first one turns steadily, but the second one speeds up and slows down twice in every turn. Machines used this when a part had to move quickly at one moment and slowly at the next.

How it works

The blue gear turns steadily. Now watch the red stripe on the orange gear. It hurries, then lags, twice in every turn. Both gears still finish each full turn together. A minus sign in the readout means the other way.

The reason is the place where the teeth touch. When a long end of the blue gear meets a flat side of the orange gear, the blue teeth are far from their shaft and the orange teeth are close to theirs. A long lever is driving a short one, so the orange gear turns faster. A quarter of a turn later it is the other way round, and the orange gear turns slower.

The two shafts never move. So where one gear sticks out, the other must be tucked in by the same amount. Try Nearly round, then Long oval. The longer the oval, the more the speed rises and falls.

In Brown's words

Elliptical spur-gears. These are used where a rotary motion of varying speed is required, and the variation of speed is determined by the relation between the lengths of the major and minor axes of the ellipses.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown draws true ellipses, each on a shaft through its middle. Two true ellipses held like that cannot stay in mesh, so the ovals here have a slightly different shape that rolls together properly. The plate that holds the shafts, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. The choice of shape was added to show what it changes.
