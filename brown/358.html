No. 358: Fusee-driven carriage

A crank turns a cone with a spiral groove, called a fusee. A band wound in the groove pulls a carriage along a track. Where the cone is wide the carriage moves fast, and where it is thin the carriage creeps, even though the crank turns steadily.

How it works

The crank turns at a steady speed. Each turn winds one loop of band onto the fusee and pulls the carriage closer. The band climbs the groove as it winds, from the wide end towards the thin tip.

One turn pulls in as much band as the distance round the groove at that spot. Round the wide end that is a long way, so the carriage moves fast. Round the thin tip it is short, so the carriage moves slowly. The carriage's speed follows the size of the cone where the band is. When the band is let out again, the spring drum pulls the carriage back.

Watch the two readouts rise and fall together. Then try Plain drum. Now the band always winds at the same size, so the carriage moves at almost one steady speed.

In Brown's words

Traverse of carriage, made variable by fusee according to the variation in diameter where the band acts.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not show what brings the carriage back, so the spring drum and its cord were added. The rails, the arm on the carriage, the sizes and the number of turns in the groove were made up. Brown's carriage has one wheel on each axle; this one has two, so it can stand on two rails. The plain drum is an extra to compare.
