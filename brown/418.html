No. 418: Buchanan and Righter's valve motion

A steam valve slides back and forth on a flat seat. Steam pushes it down hard, so it rubs and wears. Here a rod and a roller running in hung arcs hold the valve up, so it just touches the seat.

How it works

Watch the valve slide left and right. The rod leans one way, then the other. Its top pin moves up and down in the upright slot, and the red rollers run along the curved slots in the hanging plates.

Steam fills the chest and pushes the valve down onto its seat. Without help, that push makes the valve rub hard. Here the valve hangs from the rod, and the rod hangs from the rollers resting in the curved slots. The curve is exactly the path the rollers take, so the valve stays the same height all the way along.

The screw sets how high the arcs hang. Too low, and the rollers float clear, so the steam presses the valve hard. Too high, and the valve lifts off the seat and steam leaks under it. Try all three and watch the rollers and the gap. Choose Closed to see the valve chest as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Buchanan & Righter's patent slide-valve motion. Valve, A, is attached to lower end of rod, B, and free to slide horizontally on valve-seat. Upper end of rod, B, is attached to a pin which slides in vertical slots, and a roller, C, attached to the said rod, slides in two suspended and vertically adjustable arcs, D. This arrangement is intended to prevent the valve from being pressed with too great force against its seat by the pressure of steam, and to relieve it of friction.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not say what moves the valve; here a stem through the side of the chest does. The chest is cut away to show the inside. The way the arcs are hung from the screw, the guide for the top pin and all the sizes were made up.
