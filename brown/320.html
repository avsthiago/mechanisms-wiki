No. 320: Endless-chain maintaining power

A clock weight hangs in a loop of one endless rope. You can pull the rope to wind the weight back up, and the weight keeps driving the clock while you do it. So the clock never stops or loses time during winding.

How it works

The big weight hangs in a loop of rope. One side of that loop goes up over the going wheel's pulley. The other side goes up over the ratchet pulley. The holding pawl stops the ratchet pulley, so only the going wheel can give way. The weight slowly sinks and turns the going wheel.

To wind, a hand pulls down on the rope at the far left. The ratchet pulley turns the free way, and its teeth slip under the holding pawl. The rope is drawn up over it, and the big weight rises. The small weight goes down to take up the rope. The rope over the going wheel is never touched, so the weight still pulls on it the whole time.

Watch the readouts as the model winds itself every so often. The going wheel's speed never changes, even while the big weight rises. Try a faster going wheel and see the winding come round more often.

In Brown's words

Endless chain, maintaining power on going-barrel, to keep a clock going while winding, during which operation the action of the weight or main-spring is taken off the barrel. The wheel to the right is the "going-wheel," and that to the left the "striking-wheel." P is a pulley fixed to the great wheel of the going part, and roughened, to prevent a rope or chain hung over it from slipping. A similar pulley rides on another arbor, p, which may be the arbor of the great wheel of the striking part, and attached by a ratchet and click to that wheel, or to clock-frame, if there is no striking part. The weights are hung, as may be seen, the small one being only large enough to keep the rope or chain on the pulleys. If the part, b, of the rope or chain is pulled down, the ratchet-pulley runs under the click, and the great weight is pulled up by c, without taking its pressure off the going-wheel at all.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The hand that pulls the rope is left out; the rope simply runs when it is pulled. The clock train, the pendulum and the striking train are left out, so the striking wheel stands still. The holding pawl is pivoted on the striking wheel, as Brown suggests. The two crossing ropes run in two layers so they do not rub. The plate, the sizes, the tooth counts and how far the weight moves were made up.
