No. 32: Friction wheels

Two plain wheels with no teeth press rim to rim. As one turns, it rubs the other round. They run quietly and smoothly, but they only work if they grip well.

How it works

The small wheel is turned at a steady speed. Where the rims touch, it rubs the big wheel and drags it round. The two wheels turn opposite ways. A minus sign in the readout means the other way.

When the rims grip, they move past the meeting point at the same speed. The big wheel has a longer rim, so it turns more slowly. Both red stripes start at the meeting point. After a few turns they meet there again, both at once.

The driven wheel has work to do, so it pushes back. Smooth metal rims cannot grip hard enough, so they slide. That is why one rim is covered with rubber or leather. Try Rubber faced, then Bare and smooth, and watch the red stripes stop meeting.

In Brown's words

Friction-wheels. The surfaces of these wheels are made rough, so as to bite as much as possible; one is sometimes faced with leather, or, better, with vulcanized india-rubber.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows only the two wheels. The plate that holds the shafts and the sizes were made up. How much the bare wheels slip was also made up; a real pair slips more or less depending on how hard the wheels are pressed together and how much work the driven wheel does.
