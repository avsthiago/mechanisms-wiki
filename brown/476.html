No. 476: Steam siphon pump

Two pipes dip into the water and join into one pipe that rises above them. A jet of steam blows up the middle of the join and drags the water up with it. It has no moving parts to wear out or jam.

How it works

Steam comes down the thin pipe on the right, runs across inside one leg, and turns up at the fork. It shoots out of the narrow end straight up the discharge pipe. Water rises up both legs, meets at the fork and goes up too.

A fast jet drags the water next to it along. The steam meets the cold water, turns back into water and gives its push to the stream. The water it carries away is replaced from below, so a steady current flows. The steam pipe sits right in the middle of the fork and points the same way as the water, so it is not in the way.

Try a gentle jet, then a full one. Watch how fast the dots climb the legs. Choose Closed to see the pipes as they look from outside.

In Brown's words

Another apparatus operating on the same principle as the foregoing. It is termed a steam siphon pump (Lansdell's patent). A is the jet-pipe ; B, B, are two suction-pipes, having a forked connection with the discharge-pipe, C. The steam jet-pipe entering at the fork offers no obstacle to the upward passage of the water, which moves upward in an unbroken current.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front half of the pipes is cut away so the inside shows, as in the engraving. The bend of the steam pipe is simplified, and the sizes, speeds and flow numbers are made up. The dots show the water and the steam.
