No. 289: Dead-beat escapement

A weight tries to spin the toothed wheel of a clock. A rocking anchor lets one tooth pass for each swing of the pendulum. Between steps the wheel stands perfectly still and is never pushed back, so this is called a dead-beat escapement. It keeps better time than the older kind.

How it works

The weight tries to turn the wheel anticlockwise. The tip of a tooth lands on the long curved face of a pallet and rests there. The wheel cannot move.

That curved face is part of a circle drawn round the pin at the top. So as the anchor rocks, the face slides along under the tooth without pushing it back or letting it creep forward. The wheel stays dead still. When the end of the face slides out from under the tooth, the tooth runs along the short sloping end and pushes the pallet aside. That push keeps the pendulum swinging. Then a tooth on the other side lands on the other pallet.

The pendulum sets the pace. A long pendulum swings more slowly, so the beats come further apart. Try Short, then Long, and count the beats.

In Brown's words

The former is what is termed a recoil, and the latter a repose or dead-beat escapement for clocks. The same letters of reference indicate like parts in both. The anchor, H, L, K, is caused, by the oscillation of the pendulum, to vibrate upon the axis, a. Between the two extremities, or pallets, H, K, is placed the escape-wheel, A, the teeth of which come alternately against the outer surface of the pallet, K, and inner surface of pallet, H. In 289 these surfaces are cut to a curve concentric to the axis, a ; consequently, during the time one of the teeth is against the pallet the wheel remains perfectly at rest. Hence the name repose or dead-beat. In 288 the surfaces are of a differeent form, not necessary to explain, as it can be understood that any form not concentric with the axis, a, must produce a slight recoil of the wheel during the escape of the tooth, and hence the term recoil escapement. On the pallets leaving teeth, at each oscillation of the pendulum, the extremities of teeth slide along the surfaces, c, e, and d, b, and give sufficient impulse to pendulum.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The pendulum, the back plate and the sizes were made up. The pallets sit a little higher on the wheel than Brown drew them, so that the teeth meet them more squarely, and the teeth are thinner and lean further forward so that the pallets have room. The pendulum hangs in front so that it can be seen. The weight that turns the wheel is left out and its pull is taken to be steady. The teeth slide on the pallets without rubbing, and only the pendulum and the wheel are slowed a little.
