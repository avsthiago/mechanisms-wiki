No. 17: Spanish barton, second form

Two ropes and three pulleys let a small pull lift a weight five times as heavy. The rope you pull hangs a pulley on the other rope, so the other rope pulls hard too. Sailors used rigs like this to hoist heavy loads.

How it works

Follow the yellow rope up from the winch. It goes over the middle pulley and down to an arm on the lower pulley. The blue rope starts at the beam. It goes under the lower pulley, over the upper pulley, and down to hold up the middle pulley.

Say the winch pulls with 20 kg. Both sides of the yellow rope pull down on the middle pulley, so the blue rope must hold 40 kg. The lower pulley is held up by two blue parts and one yellow part. That is 40 + 40 + 20 = 100 kg.

In return, the winch pulls in 5 cm of rope for every 1 cm the weight rises. The middle pulley sinks 2 cm, because both blue parts beside the lower pulley get 1 cm shorter. Run it and compare the three readouts.

In Brown's words

Are what are known as Spanish bartons.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown ties the yellow rope to the middle of the lower pulley, so it slants. Here it is tied to a short arm so it hangs straight, which keeps the pull at exactly a fifth. Brown leaves the end of the rope hanging; a small winch was added to pull it. The rope colours, the sizes and the weight are made up. Real pulleys rub a little on their pins.
