No. 26: Crown wheel

A crown wheel has its teeth standing up from its flat face, like the points of a crown. An ordinary gear on an upright shaft meshes with them. Together they pass turning round a corner, from an upright shaft to a level one.

How it works

Look at the big wheel from the side. Its teeth do not stick out from the rim, as on most gears. They stand up from its flat face, all the way round. That is why it is called a crown wheel.

The small gear lies flat on top. Its teeth dip in between the crown teeth near the top of the wheel. Each tooth pushes a crown tooth sideways, so the crown wheel moves on by one tooth. The fewer teeth the small gear has, the more times it must turn for one turn of the crown wheel.

Brown warns that the crown teeth must be thin. They are held only at their foot, so a hard push can bend or snap them. That is why this gear is used only for light work. Try the small, medium and big gear. All three fit the same crown wheel, and the big one turns it fastest.

In Brown's words

The wheel to the right is termed a "crown-wheel;" that gearing with it is a spur-gear. These wheels are not much used, and are only available for light work, as the teeth of the crown-wheel must necessarily be thin.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The sizes, the number of teeth and the stand that holds the shafts were made up. Brown does not say which wheel drives; here the driving gear does. The choice of three gear sizes was added to show that one crown wheel fits them all.
