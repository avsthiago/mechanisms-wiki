No. 336: Marine parallel motion with rock-shaft

In a side-lever engine a long lever rocks low down, and a side rod joins its end to the top of the piston rod. The lever end swings along a curve, but the piston rod must move straight. Here the rods that keep it straight hang from short arms on a rocking shaft above the cylinder.

How it works

Watch the end of the lever. It swings along part of a circle, and its grey trace bends. The cross-head on the piston rod hangs on that end through the side rod, yet its red trace is a straight line.

The upright rod is moved at its foot by the lever, whose curve bends one way. Its top is held by the short arm, whose curve bends the other way. A point low on the upright rod moves almost straight. The box of rods copies that point's motion to the cross-head, only larger.

The Off line reading shows how far the rod strays sideways, in millimetres. A short arm makes a quick curve, so the error grows fast with a long swing. Try a short swing, then a long one, and compare the worst error. Choose Closed to see the cylinder and valve box as they look from outside.

In Brown's words

An arrangement of parallel motion for side lever marine engines. The parallel rods connected with the side rods from the beams or side levers are also connected with short radius arms on a rock-shaft working in fixed bearings.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows one lever and one set of rods; a real engine has a pair, one each side of the cylinder, with the arms on the two ends of the one rock-shaft. The heavy framing at the top left of the drawing is left out. The pillar for the rock-shaft stands on the valve box beside the cylinder. The shaft bearing, the stand under the cylinder and the floor were added. The rock-shaft was moved a little to the right of where Brown drew it, which makes the arm a little longer, so the line comes out straighter. The drawing shows the lever at the bottom of its swing. It is rocked steadily, as the engine's crank would rock it.
