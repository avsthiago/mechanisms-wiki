No. 85: Ore stamp

A heavy upright rod is lifted by curved arms on a turning shaft, then dropped. It falls by its own weight and hits the rock below. Mills like this once crushed ore to get the metal out.

How it works

The shaft turns clockwise. A wiper comes round under the block on the rod and lifts the whole stamp. Then the tip of the wiper slips out from under the block.

Nothing holds the stamp up now, so it drops by its own weight and hits the die with a bang. It lies there until the next wiper comes round. The wiper's edge is curved so it pushes straight up at a steady pace, without shoving the rod sideways.

Each wiper gives one blow every turn of the shaft. Try one wiper, then three, and count the blows in one turn.

In Brown's words

Intermittent alternating rectilinear motion is given to the rod, A, by the continuous rotation of the shaft carrying the two cams or wipers, which act upon the projection, B, of the rod, and thereby lift it. The rod drops by its own weight. Used for ore-stampers or pulverizers, and for hammers.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The die, the bearing that holds the shaft, the shape of the wipers, the sizes and the weight of the stamp were made up. The block sits in front of the rod so the wipers can pass beside it. One and three wipers were added for comparison.
