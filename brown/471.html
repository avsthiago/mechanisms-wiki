No. 471: Hotchkiss's air hammer

A crank moves a cylinder up and down. The hammer hangs from a piston inside it, held by nothing but trapped air. The air acts like a spring, so the hammer is flung down harder than the crank alone could push it.

How it works

The crank lifts and lowers the cylinder. The hammer is not tied to it. A small hole in the middle of the cylinder wall lets air in, and the piston shuts it off as it slides past.

When the cylinder goes up, the air under the piston is squeezed and lifts the hammer like a spring. When the cylinder comes down fast, the hammer lags behind, so the air above the piston is squeezed hard. Just after the crank passes the bottom, that air springs back and throws the hammer onto the anvil.

Try a slow crank: the hammer just rides along and lands softly. Then try a fast one, and watch the air above the piston and the speed of the blow. Choose Closed to see the cylinder as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Hotchkiss's atmospheric hammer; derives the force of its blow from compressed air. Hammer head, C, is attached to a piston fitted to a cylinder, B, which is connected by a rod, D, with a crank, A, on the rotary driving-shaft. As the cylinder ascends, air entering hole, e, is compressed below piston and lifts hammer. As cylinder descends, air entering hole, e, is compressed above and is stored up to produce the blow by its instant expansion after the crank and connecting-rod turn bottom center.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The cylinder is cut open at the front. The bearing behind for the crank shaft, the base, the anvil and all sizes were made up. The motion is worked out from the weight of the hammer and the push of the air on each side of the piston, squeezed as air is in a pump. It is slowed down and the air spring is set to suit the slow speed. The blue dots show the air: denser where it is squeezed.
