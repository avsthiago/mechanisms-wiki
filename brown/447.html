No. 447: Flying ferry

A boat is tied by a long rope to an anchor far upstream. With its rudder set at a slant, the river itself pushes it across, from one bank to the other. No oars and no sail are needed.

How it works

The river runs from left to right. The rope keeps the boat from drifting away, so it can only swing in a curve round the buoy. The front of the boat points upstream, turned a little toward the far bank.

The current hits the slanted side of the boat. The water pushes it partly downstream and partly sideways. The rope takes the downstream part. The sideways part swings the boat across. At the bank, the rudder is put over the other way and the boat swings back.

Try a small slant: the boat drifts part way and stops in the river. Then try 20 degrees and 30, and watch the boat cross faster.

In Brown's words

This method of passing a boat from one shore of a river to the other is common on the Rhine and elsewhere, and is effected by the action of the stream on the rudder, which carries the boat across the stream in the arc of a circle, the center of which is the anchor which holds the boat from floating down the stream.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing and description. The river is drawn a little wider than Brown's so the slanted boat fits, and the river bed, the anchor's chain and the sizes are made up. The rudder is not worked out on its own: the boat is simply held at the chosen slant, and turns round slowly at each bank. The push of the water is worked out from the current and the boat's own speed.
