No. 70: Tappet and studs with a locking rim

A wheel that turns all the time moves a second wheel on by one small step in each turn. Between steps a rim on the driving wheel locks the second wheel, so it cannot drift. Counters use steps like this.

How it works

The driving wheel turns all the time. Once in each turn the arm on its back sweeps past the stud wheel. It catches a stud and pushes it down. That moves the stud wheel on by exactly one stud.

The rest of the time a stud rests against the outside of the rim. The stud wheel cannot turn, because that stud would have to go into the rim. The gap in the rim is right in front of the arm. So the stud wheel is only free while the arm is pushing. The stud goes in through the gap and comes out again through the same gap.

So the stud wheel needs as many turns of the driver as it has studs to go round once. Try 8 studs, then 12, and watch how the rim gets bigger or smaller to fit.

In Brown's words

The driving-wheel, C, has a rim, shown in dotted outline, the exterior of which serves as a bearing and stop for the studs on the other wheel, A, when the tappet, B, is out of contact with the studs. An opening in this rim serves to allow one stud to pass in and another to pass out. The tappet is opposite the middle of this opening.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not give sizes, so the sizes of the wheels, the rim, the gap and the arm, and the upright plate that holds them were made up. The driving wheel is see-through here, as Brown draws the rim in dotted lines. The choice of 8 or 12 studs was added to show what it changes.
