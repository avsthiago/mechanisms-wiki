No. 74: Part-toothed bevel gear

A gear with teeth on only part of its rim turns two other gears, one after the other. Each one turns for a while, then waits. The two turn opposite ways, so the same steady drive gives a stop-and-go motion both ways.

How it works

The lower gear turns steadily. Only part of its rim has teeth. While the toothed part passes under the right gear, the right gear turns. When it moves on to the left gear, the left gear turns and the right one stops.

Both side gears touch the lower gear on opposite sides of it. There the rim moves one way under the right gear and the other way under the left gear. So the two gears turn opposite ways. A gear that is not being driven is held still by a little rub on its shaft.

The first tooth meets the waiting gear a little before the middle of the mesh, and the last one lets go a little after. So each gear turns a bit longer than the toothed part alone suggests. Try A third, then Two thirds. Watch when both gears rest, and when both turn at once.

In Brown's words

A uniform intermittent rotary motion in opposite directions is given to the bevel-gears, A and B, by means of the mutilated bevel-gear, C.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The stand, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. The other tooth amounts are added for comparison. The resting gears are held by rub on their shafts, not by a lock. A real part-toothed gear often has its first tooth trimmed or a locking rim so it starts more gently. Here the first tooth sets the waiting gear going at full speed at once.
