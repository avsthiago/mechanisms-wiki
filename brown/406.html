No. 406: Drawing parabolas

A straight edge, a square and a piece of cord draw a parabola, the curve of a thrown ball. The pencil always stays just as far from a pin as from the straight edge. Curved mirrors and bridge arches use this shape.

How it works

The square slides along the straight edge with its blade pointing down the page. The cord goes from the end of the blade, up along its edge to the pencil, then across to the pin. The pencil keeps the cord tight against the blade and draws one half of the curve. Then the square is turned over for the other half.

The cord is exactly as long as the blade. The part along the blade and the part to the pin always add up to that length. So the pencil is always as far from the pin as it is from the straight edge, and that makes the curve a parabola.

Try the pin near the straight edge, then far from it. A near pin gives a narrow, pointed curve and a far pin gives a wide, flat one.

In Brown's words

Mechanical means of describing parabolas, the base, altitude, focus, and directrix being given. Lay straight edge with near side coinciding with directrix, and square with stock against the same, so that the blade is parallel with the axis, and proceed with pencil in bight of thread, as in the preceding.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The paper, the sizes and the shape of the square's stock are made up. The pin is a flat-headed pin low enough for the blade to pass over it. The square is lifted and turned over at the top of the curve to draw the second half.
