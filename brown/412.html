No. 412: Capstan wheel-work

Gears hidden in the foot of a capstan let the same push wind the rope three times as hard. A bolt chooses between a quick, plain pull and a slow, strong one.

How it works

The men push bars to turn the drumhead. The spindle under it carries a small gear in the middle. Three equal gears sit round it, and they touch the teeth inside the barrel's ring.

With the gears working, the plate holding the three gears stays still. The middle gear turns them, and they turn the ring the other way. The ring has three times as many teeth as the middle gear, so the barrel goes round once for every three turns of the drumhead. The men walk three times as far, but the rope pulls three times as hard.

With the bolt dropped in, drumhead and barrel are locked together. Nothing inside can turn against anything else, so the whole set goes round as one. Try Bolted together, then Through the gears, and watch the drumhead change direction and speed against the red stripe.

In Brown's words

Wheel-work in the base of capstan. Thus provided, the capstan can be used as a simple or compound machine, single or triple purchase. The drumhead and barrel rotate independently ; the former, being fixed on spindle, turns it round, and when locked to barrel turns it also, forming single purchase ; but when unlocked, wheel-work acts, and drumhead and barrel rotate in opposite directions, with velocities as three to one.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The bars, the bolt on its arm, the holes in the barrel top, the short piece of barrel, the base and the ring track it runs on, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. Brown does not say how the gear plate is let go for the plain pull; here it simply turns with the rest. The holding pawls only rest on a plain rim, because the notches they would drop into are not drawn.
