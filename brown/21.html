No. 21: Two pulleys, ropes tied to the weight

Two ropes are tied to the weight, and both help to lift it. The lower pulley hangs from the top rope, so that rope pulls twice as hard as the winch. Together they lift 3 times the pull on the winch.

How it works

Each rope is tied to the weight at one end. It runs up over a pulley and down again. The lower rope goes to the winch. The top rope holds up the hanging pulley.

The lower rope pulls on its pulley twice, once on each side. So the top rope, which holds that pulley, pulls twice as hard as the winch. Both ropes lift the weight: one share from the lower rope and two from the top one. That makes 3 times the winch's pull.

Now watch the hanging pulley. As the weight rises, the pulley comes down to meet it just as fast. The winch winds in 3 times as far as the weight rises. Mark the top rope, then the lower one: the lower rope pulls half as hard but runs twice as fast.

In Brown's words

Are different arrangements of pulleys. The following rule applies to these pulleys:—In a system of pulleys where each pulley is embraced by a cord attached at one end to a fixed point and at the other to the center of the movable pulley, the effect of the whole will be = the number 2, multiplied by itself as many times as there are movable pulleys in the system.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown leaves the end of the rope loose; the winch was added to pull it. His rule for these figures doubles the power for each moving pulley, which would give 2 here. But his ropes are tied to the weight, so each one also lifts it directly. Counting the pulls in the drawing gives 3, and that is what the model shows. The weight and the sizes are made up. Real pulleys rub a little, so a real one needs a bit more pull.
