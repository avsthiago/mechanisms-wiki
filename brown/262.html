No. 262: Eccentric cone and roller, end view

A cone is fixed off centre on a screw, and a small roller is pressed onto it. As the screw turns, the roller speeds up and slows down once every turn. The screw also creeps along, so the roller works its way down the cone and turns slower and slower.

How it works

Seen end on, the cone looks like a big circle, and the screw passes through it below the middle. As it turns, the side under the roller swings further out, then comes back in. The roller rides up and down with it.

The roller is turned by the surface it touches. When the far side of the cone is under it, that surface is far from the screw and moves fast, so the roller turns fast. Half a turn later the near side is under it and the roller turns slowly. The screw's thread runs through a fixed nut, so the cone also moves along a little each turn. The roller ends up on a thinner part, and turns slower overall.

Watch the Roller and Height readings rise and fall together once each turn. Each fall is a little bigger than each rise, because the cone gets thinner under the roller. Try Centred, and the roller turns at a steady speed that slows only very gently.

In Brown's words

The first of these figures is an end view, and the second a side view, of an arrangement of mechanism for obtaining a series of changes of velocity and direction. D is a screw on which is placed eccentrically the cone, B, and C is a friction-roller which is pressed against the cone by a spring or weight. Continuous rotary motion, at a uniform velocity, of the screw, D, carrying the eccentric cone, gives a series of changes of velocity and direction to the roller, C. It will be understood that during every revolution of the cone the roller would press against a different part of the cone, and that it would describe thereon a spiral of the same pitch as the screw, D. The roller, C, would receive a reciprocating motion, the movement in one direction being shorter than that in the other.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The fork, the rod, the spring and the post and arm that hold them were made up; Brown only says a spring or weight presses the roller down. Brown says the roller also changes direction. With the cone only a little off centre, as he draws it, the roller never turns backwards; what goes back and forth is the roller itself, rising and falling, with each fall longer than each rise. When the roller nears the small end, the model starts the run again.
