No. 108: Crossed-groove cylinder

A cylinder has two grooves that wind round it in opposite directions, so they cross each other. A small pointer rides in the groove and slides up and down a rod. The cylinder only ever turns one way, yet the pointer goes up and then comes back down at an even speed.

How it works

Watch the red pointer. It sits in a groove on the cylinder, and the block it is fixed to can only slide up and down the rod. As the cylinder turns, the groove pushes the pointer along.

One groove winds up the cylinder like a screw thread. When it reaches the end, it curves round and winds back the other way. So the pointer is carried to the top, then back to the bottom, at the same even speed. Where the two grooves cross, the pointer keeps going straight on.

The cylinder never changes direction. Try Wide apart, then Close together. Close grooves need more turns for each trip, so the pointer moves more slowly.

In Brown's words

Uniform reciprocating rectilinear motion from uniform rotary motion of a cylinder, in which are cut reverse threads or grooves, which necessarily intersect twice in every revolution. A point inserted in the groove will traverse the cylinder from end to end.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The frame, the rod, the sliding block, the gear and all the sizes were made up. Brown shows cut grooves. Here they are drawn as dark raised bands, with the pointer resting on them. The grooves have rounded corners at the ends so the pointer turns smoothly. The choice of how far apart the grooves are was added.
