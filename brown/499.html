No. 499: Bourdon gauge

A bent metal tube tries to straighten when the pressure inside it rises. Its two free ends spread apart a little. Links, a toothed lever and a small gear turn that small movement into a big swing of the pointer.

How it works

The tube is flat, not round, and it is bent almost into a ring. It is held firmly at the top, and both of its ends are free. When steam or water is let in, the pressure tries to make the flat tube round. A flat tube that gets rounder also gets straighter, so the ring opens and its ends spread apart.

The ends move only a little. Each one pulls a link. One link pulls the top of the toothed lever and the other pulls its bottom, so both turn it the same way. The teeth are far from the lever's pin, and they turn a much smaller gear. So the pointer swings much further than the tube's ends move.

When the pressure falls, the springy metal curls back and the pointer returns. Move the pressure from None to Full and watch the gap between the ends of the tube.

In Brown's words

Aneroid gauge, known as the "Bourdon gauge," from the name of its inventor, a Frenchman. B is a bent tube closed at its ends, secured at C, the middle of its length, and having its ends free. Pressure of steam or other fluid admitted to tube tends to straighten it more or less, according to its intensity. The ends of tube are connected with a toothed sector-piece gearing, with a pinion on the spindle of a pointer which indicates the pressure on a dial.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. A real tube's ends move about a millimetre; here they move many times more, so that you can see it. The pipe behind the tube, the sizes, the number of teeth and the 0 to 10 bar scale are made up.
