No. 317: Compound-bar pendulum

The bob of this clock pendulum carries a bent bar with a small weight on each end. When it gets warm, the rod gets longer and the bob drops. But the bar bends up and lifts the weights, and that cancels the drop.

How it works

Change the temperature and watch the bar. When it is warm, the rod gets longer and the bob drops. At the same time the bar curls up at its ends and lifts the two small weights. The red line stays level with the dark line on the board.

The bar is brass below and steel on top, fixed together along their length. Brass grows more than steel when warm. The bottom gets longer than the top, so the bar has to bend upwards. The weights are small, so they must rise about five times as far as the bob drops to keep the middle of the whole weight in one place.

A pendulum keeps time by its length, so this one keeps the same time warm or cold. The real changes are far too small to see, and the model makes them much bigger. Try 5 °C, then 35 °C, and watch the weights.

In Brown's words

Compound bar compensation pendulum. C is a compound bar of brass and iron or steel, brazed together with brass downward. As brass expands more than iron, the bar will bend upward as it gets warmer, and carry the weights, W, W, up with it, raising the center of the aggregate weight, M, W, to raise the center of oscillation as much as elongation of the pendulum-rod would let it down.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which shows only the bob end of the pendulum. The rod above, its spring, the bracket and the back board were made up, and the rod is drawn much shorter than a real one. The weights are taken as a quarter as heavy as the bob. The bending and the changes in length are made far larger than real ones so that you can see them. The readouts are worked out for a steel rod about a metre long.
