No. 423: Root's double-quadrant engine

A steam engine with two swinging pistons in one case. Each piston is a flat blade on a pivot, and steam pushes on one side of it only. Both blades are joined to the same crank, and their pushes overlap, so the crank never sticks.

How it works

Watch the two blades. The top one swings down and up, and the bottom one swings up and down. Each pulls its rod, and both rods turn the one crank in the middle.

Steam fills the space behind a blade and pushes it away from its end wall. Steam can only push, never pull, so each blade drives the crank only while it moves away from its wall. That lasts a little more than half a turn of the crank. On the way back, the used steam is pushed out into the middle of the case.

Watch the blue dots of steam and the readout. Most of the time one blade is pushing. For a short while in each turn both push together, and there is never a moment when neither pushes. So the crank has no dead points. Choose Closed to see the engine as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Root's patent double-quadrant engine. This is on the same principle as 422 ; but two single-acting pistons, B, B, are used, and both connected with one crank, D. The steam is admitted to act on the outer sides of the two pistons alternately by means of one induction valve, a, and is exhausted through the space between the pistons. The piston and crank connections are such that the steam acts on each piston during about two-thirds of the revolution of the crank, and hence there are no dead points.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front of the case is cut away so the blades show, and the rods and crank are drawn in front of it. The legs and base, the exact shape of the case and channels, and all the sizes were made up. The valve is shown turning with the crank, but how it is driven and timed was made up. The blue dots show the steam. Dark dots are fresh steam pushing, and pale dots are used steam leaving.
