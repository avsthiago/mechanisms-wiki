No. 429: Holly's elliptical rotary engine

Two oval pistons with gear teeth turn inside a casing shaped like a figure eight. Steam comes in between them and pushes them round in opposite ways. The teeth keep the two ovals in step, so they always seal against each other.

How it works

Look at the two ovals. Each turns on its own shaft, and their teeth lock together in the middle. When one turns clockwise, the other must turn anticlockwise. A long end of one always meets a flat side of the other, so no gap opens between them.

Steam comes in at the top, shown as dark blue dots. It cannot go straight down the middle, because the teeth block the way. So it pushes the ovals round. Each oval carries a pocket of steam round the outside wall to the bottom. The pale dots at the bottom are used steam going out.

Watch the two readouts. The left piston turns steadily, but the right one speeds up and slows down twice in each turn. That is because the place where the teeth meet moves nearer to one shaft, then nearer to the other. Now let the steam in at the bottom. Both pistons turn the other way. Choose Closed to see the engine as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Holly's patent double-elliptical rotary engine. The two elliptical pistons geared together are operated upon by the steam entering between them, in such manner as to produce their rotary motion in opposite directions. These rotary engines can all be converted into pumps.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front cover is left off, and the steam is drawn as small blue balls so you can see it move. Brown draws nearly round pistons with two long teeth each. Here they are ovals with teeth all the way round, shaped so that they roll together exactly, and their long ends do the sealing. The model turns the left piston at a steady speed. In a real engine both would speed up and slow down a little. Brown shows no foot, so the casing hangs on its two pipes. The sizes, the number of teeth and the choice of pipe were made up.
