No. 422: Oscillating piston engine

A steam engine whose piston swings instead of sliding. The cylinder is shaped like a slice of pie, and the piston is a flat blade fixed to a shaft at the point. Steam pushes the blade one way, then the other, and the rocking shaft turns a crank.

How it works

Watch the blade inside the cylinder. It swings from one side to the other, like a door that never opens all the way. The arm on its shaft swings up and down and turns the crank through the rod.

Steam comes in at the top. The valve sends it down one channel to a far corner of the cylinder. There it fills the space behind the blade and pushes it across. Meanwhile the steam on the other side flows out through the other channel. Then the valve slides over and it all happens the other way.

Watch the valve and the blue dots of steam together. Each time the blade reaches the end of its swing, the valve has moved to send steam to the other side. Choose Closed to see the engine as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Oscillating piston engine. The profile of the cylinder A, is of the form of a sector. The piston, B, is attached to a rock-shaft, C, and steam is admitted to the cylinder to operate on one and the other side of piston alternately, by means of a slide-valve, D, substantially like that of an ordinary reciprocating engine. The rock-shaft is connected with a crank to produce rotary motion.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front of the cylinder is cut away so the blade shows. Brown only says the rocking shaft is joined to a crank, so the arm, rod, crank, the post that holds it and the base were made up, as were all the sizes. The rod that moves the valve is left unjoined; here the valve moves as if it were worked from the crank shaft. The blue dots show the steam. Dark dots are fresh steam pushing, and pale dots are used steam leaving.
