No. 394: Parsons's endless rack

A steam piston pushes a long frame in and out. Teeth all round the inside of the frame turn a small gear, always the same way. So the in and out push becomes steady turning, with no crank.

How it works

Steam pushes the piston, and the piston rod pushes the frame. The frame has teeth all round the inside of its opening. The small gear, called a pinion, sits inside and meshes with the upper row.

As the frame moves one way, the upper teeth turn the pinion. At the end of the stroke the pinion rolls round the curved end and meets the lower row. Now the frame comes back, and the lower teeth turn the pinion the same way as before. The frame must rise and fall a little for this, so the cylinder rocks on two pins. Two flanges behind the pinion run in a groove on the frame and keep the teeth from slipping out.

Try Anticlockwise, then Clockwise. See which way the frame moves while the pinion is on the upper row. Choose Closed to see the cylinder as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

C. Parsons's patent device for converting reciprocating motion into rotary, an endless rack provided with grooves on its side gearing with a pinion having two concentric flanges of different diameters. A substitute for crank in oscillating cylinder engines.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's description and drawing. Brown shows only the frame, the pinion and the rod. The rocking cylinder, its posts, the bearing and base, and all sizes and numbers of teeth were made up. How the two flanges sit in the groove is our guess: the big one runs against the outer wall and the small one against the inner wall. The pinion is turned at a steady speed for you; in a real engine the steam does the pushing.
