No. 451: Force pump with an air chamber

A force pump only pushes water out on the down stroke, so the water comes in spurts. Here the water goes first into a closed dome with air trapped in it. The squeezed air keeps pushing while the piston goes back up, so the water flows out steadily.

How it works

Watch the piston go down. It pushes water along the passage, past the flap and into the dome. The water has nowhere to go fast enough, so it rises in the dome and squeezes the air above it.

When the piston goes back up, the flap shuts. The squeezed air springs back and keeps pushing water out of the two pipes, so the jets hardly drop between strokes. After a few strokes the air settles at a steady squeeze, and the water rises and falls only a little in the dome.

A lot of air acts like a soft cushion. A little air is stiff, so it squeezes and springs back quickly. Try a full dome, then a little air, and watch the jets start to come in spurts. Choose Closed to see the pump as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Force pump, same as above, with addition of air-chamber to the outlet, to produce a constant flow. The outlet from air-chamber is shown at two places, from either of which water may be taken. The air is compressed by the water during the downward stroke of the piston, and expands and presses out the water from the chamber during the up-stroke.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which is a cut-away. The front half of the pump is left off so you can see in. The passage to the dome is drawn as one simple pipe, and the dome's waist is left out. The sizes, the shape of the valves, how easily water leaves the pipes and the made-up size used for the litres are not from the book. The pipes are cut short, and the model starts with the pump full of water and the air already squeezed.
