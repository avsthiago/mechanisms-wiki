No. 248: Union coupling

A union joins two pipes so they can be taken apart again. A nut screws onto one pipe and presses the flange of the other pipe against it. The picture is cut in half so you can see inside.

How it works

The picture is cut in half, so you see the walls as flat surfaces. Look at the zigzag where the nut meets the lower pipe. That is the screw thread. Each turn of the nut moves it up or down by one thread.

When the nut is screwed down, its lip pushes on the flange. The flange pushes on the end of the lower pipe, so the two pipes are pressed together. When the nut is unscrewed, the upper pipe is free and can be lifted away.

Follow the red marks as the nut turns. Count how many turns it takes to free the pipe. A coarser thread would need fewer turns, and a finer one more. Choose Closed to see the joint as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Union coupling. A is a pipe with a small flange abutting against the pipe, C, with a screwed end; B a nut which holds them together.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the joint cut in half with the nut screwed tight. The sizes, the thread depth and the number of threads were made up, and the thread is drawn as rings, not a spiral. The red marks, the small gap and the lifting of the upper pipe by hand were added.
