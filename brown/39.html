No. 39: Sun and planet

A rod pushed up and down by an engine turns a shaft round and round, without a crank. A gear on the end of the rod rolls round a gear on the shaft. The shaft turns twice for every trip the rod's gear makes round it.

How it works

The rod goes up and down, and its gear is carried round the sun gear. The arm holds the two centres together. The rod leans a little, but its gear never turns right round.

Think of the planet gear as standing still while it is carried round. Its teeth roll along the sun's teeth like a wheel along a curved road. One trip round carries the sun gear round once. The rolling of the teeth adds one more turn. So the sun gear turns twice for each trip.

Watch the readouts. With equal gears the sun makes two turns for each turn of the arm. Try Half as big and compare. The extra turn from rolling is now only half a turn.

In Brown's words

Sun-and-planet motion. The spur-gear to the right, called the planet-gear, is tied to the center of the other, or sun-gear, by an arm which preserves a constant distance between their centers. This was used as a substitute for the crank in a steam engine by James Watt, after the use of the crank had been patented by another party. Each revolution of the planet-gear, which is rigidly attached to the connecting-rod, gives two to the sun-gear, which is keyed to the fly-wheel shaft.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the two gears, the arm and the bottom of the rod. The slotted ring round the sun gear is drawn here as a flywheel behind the gears. The guide at the top of the rod, the plate and base, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. In a real engine the rod hangs from the end of a rocking beam instead of a straight guide. The choice of planet size was added to show where the second turn comes from.
