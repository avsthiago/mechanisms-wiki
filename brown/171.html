No. 171: Oscillating engine reversing gear

Two rods, each driven by its own eccentric, rock a curved link. A block in the link works a slide, and the slide moves the valve of the engine. Moving the block to one end of the link makes the engine run one way, and moving it to the other end makes it run backwards.

How it works

The shaft turns two round discs that are fixed off-centre, called eccentrics. Each disc pushes its own rod up and down. The two rods hold the ends of a curved link, so the link rocks. Watch the red block.

The block sits in the slot of the link and rides on a straight rod. With the block at one end of the slot, it copies the rod at that end. With it at the other end, it copies the other rod, and the valve works the opposite way round. In the middle the two rods cancel out and the valve hardly moves. The rod pushes a curved slide, and a pin in the slide's slot carries the motion to the valve. The slot is curved round the trunnion, so the engine can swing without moving the pin.

Try Left end, then Middle, then Right end. Watch the red block and the engine readout.

In Brown's words

Valve motion and reversing gear used in oscillating marine engines. The two eccentric rods give an oscillating motion to the slotted link which works the curved slide over the trunnion. Within the slot in the curved slide is a pin attached to the arm of a rock-shaft which gives motion to the valve. The curve of the slot in the slide is an arc of a circle described from the center of the trunnion, and as it moves with the cylinder it does not interfere with the stroke of the valve. The two eccentrics and link are like those of the link motion used in locomotives.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The frame, the posts, the pillar for the rock-shaft, the sizes of the discs and the handle on the link were made up. Brown's lever runs off the drawing, so here it is a short handle fixed to the link. The swing of the cylinder on its trunnion is not shown. The arm that works the valve is drawn straight.
