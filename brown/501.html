No. 501: Mercury barometer

A bent glass tube holds a column of mercury, a heavy liquid metal. The air pressing on the open end holds the column up. When the air presses harder the column rises, so the tube shows changes in the weather.

How it works

Look at the two mercury surfaces. The one in the short side is open to the air, and the air pushes down on it. The one in the long side has nothing above it, not even air. So the air pushes the mercury round the bend and up the long side.

The mercury climbs until its weight balances the push of the air. On a normal day that takes about 760 mm of mercury, measured from the lower surface to the upper one. When the air pushes harder the column grows, and when it pushes less the column sinks. Falling pressure often brings rain.

Both surfaces move, by the same amount in opposite ways. So the marks on the scale are set half as far apart as real centimetres. Try Storm, then Fine. Then try Mountain top, where there is much less air above you.

In Brown's words

Mercurial barometer. Longer leg of bent tube, against which is marked the scale of inches, is closed at top, and shorter one is open to the atmosphere, or merely covered with some porous material. Column of mercury in longer leg, from which the air has been extracted, is held up by the pressure of air on the surface of that in the shorter leg, and rises or falls as the pressure of the atmosphere varies. The old-fashioned weather-glass is composed of a similar tube attached to the back of a dial, and a float inserted into the shorter leg of the tube, and geared by a rack and pinion, or cord and pulley, with the spindle of the pointer.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. It is drawn to scale, so changes in the weather move the mercury only a little. The board, the clips and the width of the tube are made up, and the open end is shown with a cloth cover. Brown's scale is in inches; this one is in centimetres.
