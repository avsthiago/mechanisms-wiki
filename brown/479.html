No. 479: Gasometer

A big upside-down cup floats in a tank of water and holds gas. When gas flows in, the cup rises. When gas flows out, it sinks. Weights on ropes carry part of the cup's weight, and they decide how hard the gas is squeezed.

How it works

Gas goes into the bell through one pipe. The bell has no bottom, so the gas pushes up on the roof and the bell rises. When gas leaves by the other pipe, the bell sinks. The water seals the rim in every position.

Look at the water inside the bell. It sits lower than the water outside. The gas has to push the water down to make room, and the gap shows how hard the gas is squeezed. That squeeze is the weight the gas carries, spread over the roof.

The balls take weight off the gas. Try small balls, then big ones. With big balls the bell is lighter, the squeeze is gentler and the water inside sits nearly level with the water outside. Choose Closed to see the gasometer as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Gasometer. The open-bottomed vessel, A, is arranged in the tank, B, of water, and partly counterbalanced by weights, C, C. Gas enters the gasometer by one and leaves it by the other of the two pipes inserted through the bottom of the tank. As gas enters, vessel, A, rises, and vice versa. The pressure is regulated by adding to or reducing the weights, C, C.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The tank is round and cut open at the front so you can see inside. The posts that hold the pulleys, the sizes, the rope lengths and the three ball sizes are made up. The water level outside is kept steady, and the gas dots only show how much gas is in the bell.
