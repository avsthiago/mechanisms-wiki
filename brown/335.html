No. 335: Beam engine parallel motion

The end of a rocking beam moves along a curve, but a piston rod must go straight up and down. This set of links joins the two. It turns the curve into a line that is very nearly straight.

How it works

Watch the end of the beam. It swings up and down along part of a circle, and its grey trace bends. Now watch the red piston rod. Its trace is a straight line.

Look at the back link, the one nearer the middle of the beam. Its top is carried along the beam's curve, which bends one way. Its bottom is held by the radius rod, whose curve bends the other way. A point part way down that link moves almost straight. The box of links copies that point's motion onto the piston rod, only larger.

No set of rods can make a perfectly straight line this way. The Off line reading shows how far the rod strays sideways, in millimetres. Try a short swing, then a long one, and compare the worst error. Choose Closed to see the cylinder as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

A parallel motion commonly used for stationary beam engines.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows only the end of the beam and the links. The rest of the beam's half, its pillar, the post for the fixed pin, the cylinder and the floor were added so it stands up. The links are drawn on the front of the beam only; real engines had a pair, one on each side. Lengths were measured from the drawing, then the back link was moved a little so the line comes out as straight as it can. The beam is rocked steadily, as a crank and flywheel would rock it.
