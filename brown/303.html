No. 303: Dead-beat pendulum escapement

A weight tries to spin the toothed wheel of a clock. Two pallets on a rocking frame let one tooth pass for each swing of the pendulum. Between steps the wheel stands perfectly still and is never pushed back. That is why it is called dead-beat, and why it keeps good time.

How it works

The weight tries to turn the wheel clockwise. The tip of a tooth lands on the curved face of a pallet and rests there. The wheel cannot move.

Each resting face is part of a circle drawn round the pin at the top. On the left pallet it is the outer face, and on the right pallet it is the inner face. So as the frame rocks, the face slides past the tooth without pushing it back or letting it creep forward. The wheel stays dead still. When the end of the face slides clear, the tooth runs along the short sloping end and pushes the pallet aside. That push keeps the pendulum swinging. Then a tooth on the other side lands on the other pallet.

The pendulum sets the pace. A long pendulum swings more slowly, so the beats come further apart. Try Short, then Long, and count the beats.

In Brown's words

A dead-beat pendulum escapement. The inner face of the pallet, E, and outer face of D, are concentric with the axis on which the pallets vibrate, and hence there is no recoil.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing and description. The back plate, the base, the bob and the sizes were made up. The teeth are wedges with a narrow flat tip, where Brown drew square pegs, and the pallets are slimmer, so that a pallet has room to drop in between two teeth. Brown draws the pendulum twice, hanging straight and swung to one side. Here it is one pendulum that swings. It hangs in front so that it can be seen. The weight that turns the wheel is left out and its pull is taken to be steady. The teeth slide on the pallets without rubbing, and only the pendulum and the wheel are slowed a little.
