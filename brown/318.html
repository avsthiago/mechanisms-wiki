No. 318: Watch regulator

A small lever on a watch lets you make it run faster or slower. Two pins on the lever hold the coiled balance spring. Moving the lever changes how much of the spring is free to bend.

How it works

The balance wheel swings to and fro, and the coiled spring winds and unwinds with it. The spring always pulls the wheel back towards the middle, so it keeps swinging. Each swing lets the watch move on one tick.

The two pins on the lever pinch the outer turn of the spring. The spring can only bend between the wheel and the pins; past the pins it is held still. Move the lever to the right and the pins slide along the spring towards its middle. Less spring is free to bend, so it is stiffer, and a stiffer spring swings the wheel faster.

A small move makes a big difference to a watch, because it ticks hundreds of times a minute all day long. Try Slow, then Fast, and watch how much the watch gains or loses in a day.

In Brown's words

Watch regulator. The balance-spring is attached at its outer end to a fixed stud, R, and at its inner end to staff of balance. A neutral point is formed in the spring at P by inserting it between two curb-pins in the lever, which is fitted to turn on a fixed ring concentric with staff of balance, and the spring only vibrates between this neutral point and staff of balance. By moving lever to the right, the curb-pins are made to reduce the length of acting part of spring, and the vibrations of balance are made faster ; and by moving it to the left an opposite effect is produced.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The back plate, the bridge that holds the stud and the ring, and the sizes were made up. The wheel swings far more slowly than a real one so that you can follow it. The rate is worked out from the free length of the spring alone, and the scale on the plate has no real units.
