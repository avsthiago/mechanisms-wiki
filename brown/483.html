No. 483: Dry gas meter

This meter counts gas without any water inside. Gas fills two pairs of leather bellows in turn, pushing their plates back and forth. The plates work a valve that sends the gas to the next bellows, and every fill is counted.

How it works

Each moving plate splits its side into two leather bags. The valve lets gas into one bag, and the gas pushes the plate along. The bag on the other side of the plate is squeezed, and its gas goes out to the house. Then the valve swaps them over.

The plates cannot turn anything by themselves, so each one rocks an upright rod behind it. Links from the tops of the two rods turn the crank, and the crank turns the valve. The two sides move a quarter turn apart, so one plate is always pushing hard while the other turns round at the end of its stroke.

Each bag holds a known amount of gas, so every fill counts the same. Change the gas flow: the plates move faster, but the gas counted for each fill stays the same.

In Brown's words

Dry gas meter. Consists of two bellows-like chambers, A, A', which are alternately filled with gas, and discharged through a valve, B, something like the slide-valve of a steam engine, worked by the chambers, A, A'. The capacity of the chambers being known, and the number of times they are filled being registered by dial-work, the quantity of gas passing through the meter is indicated on the dials.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown's valve is a slide valve; here it is drawn as a round valve that turns, and its passages are not shown. The arms, the links, the bridge over the valve, the sizes and the amount of gas per turn are made up. The counter is left out; the readouts do its job.
