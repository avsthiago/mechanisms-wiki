No. 468: Flexible water main

A water pipe made of short stiff sections joined by ball-and-socket joints can bend at every joint. Laid across a river bed, it bends to follow the bed and still carries water without leaking.

How it works

The main is a chain of short, stiff pipes. At each joint, a ball sits inside a socket, so one pipe can tilt a little against the next. Each pipe lies on two logs, and iron plates and a pin at each joint let the logs swing too.

The bed under the river is not flat. Each joint turns just enough for the pipes to lie on the bed. No single pipe bends, but the whole line does. Water keeps flowing through every joint.

Brown says the line was built on one bank and hauled across the river. Bending let it follow the bed as it went. Press a bed shape to see the pipes find a new line, and compare the biggest bend in each.

In Brown's words

Flexible water main, plan and section. Two pipes of 15 and 18 inches interior diameter, having some of their joints thus formed, conduct water across the Clyde to Glasgow Water-works. Pipes are secured to strong log frames, having hinges with horizontal pivots. Frames and pipes were put together on south side of the river, and, the north end of pipe being plugged, they were hauled across by machinery on north side, their flexible structure enabling them to follow the bed.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. It shows the side view, as in the upper picture. The number of sections, their sizes, the logs, the straps, the hinge plates and the bed under them are made up, and the bed always has exactly the slope the pipe needs. The water is drawn as moving dots.
