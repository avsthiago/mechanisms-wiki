No. 282: Slotted bar, sector and racks

A turning disc rocks a long upright bar from side to side. Its bottom end slides a toothed bar back and forth. Its top end pulls a cord that lifts and lowers a weight. One steady turn becomes two to-and-fro motions at once.

How it works

The pin on the disc goes round in a circle. It sits in a long slot in the bar, so it can slide along the bar but not across it. Each time it swings to one side, it pushes the bar over with it. The bar rocks to and fro on the pin near its foot.

The bar's two ends move opposite ways. When the top leans left, the fan of teeth at the bottom swings right and rolls the rack to the right. At the same time the top end pulls the cord away from the pulley and the weight rises. The top is much farther from the pivot than the teeth, so it travels much farther.

The disc turns at the same speed all the time, but the bar does not. It moves fastest when the pin passes the middle and slows to a stop at each end. Try the inner hole, then the outer one, and compare how far the rack and the weight travel.

In Brown's words

By the revolution of the disk in which is fixed a pin working in a slot in the upright bar which turns on a center near the bottom, both ends of the bar are made to traverse, the toothed sector producing alternate rectilinear motion in the horizontal bar at the bottom, and also alternate perpendicular motion of the weight.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The rails that carry the disc's shaft and the bar's pivot, the guides for the rack and the bracket for the pulley were made up. Brown shows only one pin hole in the disc; the other two were added for the control. The curved slot in the top of the frame is read as a guide for a pin on the bar. The weight hangs a little lower than drawn so that it never reaches the pulley.
