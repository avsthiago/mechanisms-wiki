No. 493: Lewis for lifting stone

A lewis is a little tool that lets a crane pick up a rough stone. It fits into a hole cut in the top of the stone. Lifting on it makes it grip the sides of the hole, so the stone comes up. To let go, you set the stone down and take the tool out.

How it works

The wedge and the two packing pieces go into the slot together. At first they sit loosely. The rope pulls the wedge up. Its wide bottom squeezes in between the packing pieces and pushes them out against the sides of the slot.

Now the pieces are jammed hard against the stone. The harder the rope pulls, the harder the pieces press, so the stone comes up with the rope. A heavy stone only makes the grip stronger.

Set the stone down and slack off the rope. The wedge sinks and the grip goes. Then the whole lewis lifts out of the slot, ready for the next stone. Watch the readout and see when it is gripping.

In Brown's words

"Lewis," for lifting stone in building. It is composed of a central taper pin or wedge, with two wedge-like packing-pieces arranged one on each side of it. The three pieces are inserted together in a hole drilled into the stone, and when the central wedge is hoisted upon it wedges the packing-pieces out so tightly against the sides of the hole as to enable the stone to be lifted.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which is a section. The stone is cut away at its two faces so the lewis can be seen inside the slot. The size of the slot, how far the wedge has to move, the shackle, the rope and the ground were made up. In the model the packing pieces hang on the wedge when the lewis is taken out. The lifting and setting down are not driven by a real crane.
