No. 183: Hand-gear with quadrants

Two handles work the steam valves of a big pumping engine. The piston rod knocks them over by itself at each end of its stroke. Two smooth quadrants, one on each handle's shaft, lock each handle until the other one is knocked over.

How it works

The piston rod moves up and down beside two handles. It carries two red blocks called tappets. Each handle turns a shaft, and a crank on that shaft raises or lowers a valve rod. Each shaft also carries a quadrant, a flat fan with a smooth curved edge.

Here the piston is rising, with steam below it. The pin of the upper quadrant rests on the curved edge of the lower one, so the upper handle cannot move. Near the top, the lower tappet lifts the lower handle and shuts the steam below. Its curved edge slides away under the pin. The upper handle is free now, so its back weight swings it up and lets steam in above. Now the lower handle is held up by the upper quadrant.

The edges are curved round each shaft, so pressing on them cannot turn them. That is what makes the lock safe. Set the speed to the lowest and watch each pin slide along the other quadrant's edge and drop off its end.

In Brown's words

Represent a modification of 181 and 182, the diagonal catches being superseded by two quadrants.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The wall, the guides, the back weights and where they hang are made up. The quadrants' sizes and shapes are guesses, and each one carries a small pin that rests on the other. Brown speaks of one tappet. Here the rod has two, one for each handle, so it never has to pass a handle on the way. The valves are not shown, and the piston is moved by steam in a simple way, with no real engine behind it.
