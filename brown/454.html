No. 454: Diaphragm forcing pump

A pump with no piston. A sheet of flexible stuff, called a diaphragm, is clamped across the top of the chamber. Pulling its middle up draws water in, and pushing it down forces water out up the pipe.

How it works

Watch the middle of the diaphragm. When the handle lifts it, the space under it grows. Water pushes up the lifting valve at the bottom and fills the chamber. The flap on the right stays shut, so nothing comes back down the delivery pipe.

When the handle pushes it down, the space shrinks. The bottom valve drops shut, so the water has only one way to go. It lifts the flap in the bulge and is forced up the pipe on the right.

The edge of the diaphragm never moves, so nothing has to slide or rub to keep the water in. Try pausing halfway down a stroke and follow the dots through the open flap. Choose Closed to see the pump as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Diaphragm forcing pump. A flexible diaphragm is employed instead of bellows, and valves are arranged same as in preceding.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front half of the pump is cut away so the inside shows. The body is drawn as a flat box so all the passages show at once, and the diaphragm bends only from side to side. The shape of the handle and bracket, the guide for the lifting valve and all the sizes were made up. The litres count assumes a made-up size for the chamber.
