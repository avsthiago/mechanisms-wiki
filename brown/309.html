No. 309: Mudge's gravity escapement

A weight tries to turn the toothed wheel. Two weighted arms, called pallets, take turns to stop it. The wheel lifts each arm a little, and the arm then pushes the pendulum with its own weight as it falls back. The wheel never touches the pendulum, so every push is the same.

How it works

Each pallet is an arm with a ball weight, turning on its own pin at the top. Under the arm is a sloping face that ends in a small hook. A tooth of the wheel slides along the slope and lifts the arm. Then the tooth runs into the hook, and the wheel stops.

A long tail hangs from each pallet down to a pin beside the pendulum. The pendulum swings out, meets the pin and lifts the arm a little more. The hook comes clear of the tooth. The wheel jumps on and lifts the other arm. Then the pendulum swings back and the first arm falls with it. No tooth holds it up now, so it falls further than it was carried. That extra fall is the push that keeps the pendulum swinging.

The push comes from the weight of the pallet, not from the wheel. Try Weak, then Strong. The wheel jumps faster, but the pendulum swings just as far as before. A clock like this keeps good time even when the pull on its wheel changes.

In Brown's words

Mudge's gravity escapement. The pallets, A, B, instead of being on one arbor, are on two, as shown at C. The pendulum plays between the fork-pins, P, Q, and so raises one of the weighted pallets out of the wheel at each vibration. When the pendulum returns the pallet falls with it, and the weight of the pallet gives the impulse.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not draw the pendulum, so the rod, the bar across it that meets the two tail pins, and the bob were made up. The tails hang closer together than drawn. The exact shape of the slopes and hooks, the back plate, the rest pins, the base and all the sizes were made up. The weight that turns the wheel is left out. Nothing rubs in this model. Only the air slows the pendulum and the wheel.
