No. 467: Hydrostatic jack

A jack for lifting heavy loads with water. The ram in the middle stands still, and the cylinder slides up over it, carrying a claw. A small hand pump pushes water into the cylinder above the ram, and a screw lets it out again.

How it works

Watch the lever on the left rock up and down. Its plunger draws water from the base and pushes it up the pipe in the middle of the ram. The water fills the space between the top of the ram and the top of the cylinder, and pushes the cylinder up.

The plunger is thin and the ram is fat, so each stroke lifts the cylinder only a tiny bit. That is what makes it strong. The small push on the plunger is spread over the much bigger top of the ram, so the cylinder is pushed up many times harder.

When the cylinder is high, the screw at the bottom is turned and the water runs back into the base. Try a crack, then wide open, and compare how gently the load comes down. Choose Closed to see the jack as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Robertson's hydrostatic jack. In this the ram is stationary upon a hollow base and the cylinder with claw attached slides upon it. The pump takes the water from the hollow base and forces it through a pipe in the ram into the cylinder, and so raises the latter. At the bottom of pipe there is a valve operated by a thumb-screw to let back the water and lower the load as gradually as may be desired.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. It is cut open down the middle and the water is shown pale blue, with moving dots where it flows. How the pump fits on the base, the post for the lever and the pipes inside the base were made up. Brown's drawing also shows a long handle on the side of the cylinder; here it is taken as a handle for carrying the jack. The pump valves are left out. All sizes were made up, and the cylinder rises by the volume of water pushed in, with no leaks.
