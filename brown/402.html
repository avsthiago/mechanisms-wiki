No. 402: Guernsey's twin-balance escapement

A watch keeps time with a balance wheel that swings to and fro. A sudden knock can speed the balance up or slow it down. This watch has two balances that always swing opposite ways, so a knock that helps one hinders the other just as much.

How it works

The escape wheel is always trying to turn. One pallet of the anchor sits in the way of a tooth and holds it. As the anchor rocks, that pallet lifts clear. The tooth slides off its sloping end and gives the anchor a small push. Then the other pallet catches the next tooth.

The lever is fixed to the anchor, so it rocks too. The curved bar at its end is a piece of a gear with teeth on both edges. The upper balance has its small gear inside the curve and the lower balance has its small gear outside. When the bar swings, one small gear is turned clockwise and the other anticlockwise.

Now think of a knock that twists the whole watch. It tries to turn both balances the same way. But the gear teeth only let them turn opposite ways, so the two pushes cancel and the watch keeps its pace. The springs set that pace. Try Soft, then Stiff, and count the beats.

In Brown's words

G. O. Guernsey's patent escapement for watches. In this escapement two balance-wheels are employed, carried by the same driving-power, but oscillating in opposite directions, for the purpose of counteracting the effect of any sudden jar upon a watch or time-piece. The jar which would accelerate motion of one wheel would retard the motion of other. Anchor, A, is secured to lever, B, having an interior and exterior toothed segment at its end, each one of which gears with the pinion of balance-wheels.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The coiled springs, the back plate, the tooth numbers and the sizes were made up. The drawing does not show the shape of the pallets, so they are the common kind with a round catch face and a sloping end. The mainspring is left out and its push on the wheel is taken to be steady. The lever, the gear teeth and the two balances are taken as one body that swings together. The parts slide on each other without rubbing, and only that body and the wheel are slowed a little.
