No. 19: Pulleys above and on the weight

Three ropes each loop under a small pulley on the weight, so each rope lifts it twice. The pulleys above hang in a chain, and each rope pulls three times as hard as the one below it. Together they lift 26 times the pull on the winch.

How it works

Each rope is tied to the frame of a pulley above. It runs down under a small pulley on the weight, back up over the pulley above, and down again. The bottom rope goes to the winch. The other two each hold up the next pulley down.

The bottom rope pulls on its pulley three times: once on each side of the wheel and once where it is tied. So the rope holding that pulley pulls 3 times as hard as the winch. One pulley up, the same thing makes it 9 times. Each rope lifts the weight twice, so the weight gets 2 + 6 + 18 = 26 times the winch's pull.

The price is a lot of rope. The winch winds in 26 times as far as the weight rises, so the weight hardly moves. The lower pulley races down four times as fast as the middle one. Mark the top rope, then the bottom one: each rope down the chain pulls a third as hard but runs three times as fast.

In Brown's words

Are different arrangements of pulleys. The following rule applies to these pulleys:—In a system of pulleys where each pulley is embraced by a cord attached at one end to a fixed point and at the other to the center of the movable pulley, the effect of the whole will be = the number 2, multiplied by itself as many times as there are movable pulleys in the system.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown leaves the end of the rope loose; the winch was added to pull it. His rule for these figures doubles the power for each moving pulley. That does not fit this drawing, because the small pulleys on the weight make each rope lift it twice. Counting the pulls in the drawing gives 26, and that is what the model shows. The weight and the sizes are made up. Real pulleys rub a little, so a real one needs a bit more pull.
