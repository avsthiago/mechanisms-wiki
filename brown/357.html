No. 357: Gyroscope governor

A governor keeps a steam engine at a steady speed. This one uses a heavy spinning wheel in place of the usual flying balls. The faster the engine runs, the harder the wheel tries to stand upright, and that lifts a rod that closes the steam valve.

How it works

Watch the small gear on the left, called a pinion. The frame carries it round the ring of teeth, so it has to roll. That turns the axle, and the axle spins the heavy wheel. The wheel turns several times for each turn of the frame.

The wheel is spinning and is being carried round at the same time. A spinning wheel that is swung round like this tries to tip over, so that it lies level and spins the same way as the frame. Here that pushes the leaning wheel towards upright. The spring pushes back through the lever and the rods. The faster the engine runs, the stronger the tipping push, and the more it squeezes the spring.

Try the engine speed slider. Go from slow to fast and see the wheel stand up, the valve rod rise and the spring shorten. On a real engine the rising rod closes the steam valve a little, and the engine slows down again.

In Brown's words

What is called the gyroscope governor, for steam engines, etc., patented by Alban Anderson in 1858. A is a heavy wheel, the axle, B, B', of which is made in two pieces connected together by a universal joint. The wheel, A, is on one piece, B, and a pinion, I, on the other piece, B'. The piece, B, is connected at its middle by a hinge joint with the revolving frame, H, so that variations in the inclination of the wheel, A, will cause the outer end of the piece, B, to rise and fall. The frame, H, is driven by bevel gearing from the engine, and by that means the pinion, I, is carried round the stationary toothed circle, G, and the wheel, A, is thus made to receive a rapid rotary motion on its axis. When the frame, H, and wheel, A, are in motion, the tendency of the wheel, A, is to assume a vertical position, but this tendency is opposed by a spring, L. The greater the velocity of the governor, the stronger is the tendency above mentioned, and the more it overcomes the force of the spring, and vice versa. The piece, B, is connected with the valve-rod by rods, C, D, and the spring, L, is connected with the said rod by levers, N, and rod, P.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing and description. The drawing does not show clearly how the wheel's axle is hinged, so here it tips about the middle of the universal joint, in a small cradle. The cradle, the collar on the end of the axle, the shape of the lever and the plain square stand were made up, and so were all the sizes and the numbers of teeth. The lean is worked out from the spin of the wheel, its weight and the spring. The pieces other than the wheel are taken to weigh nothing, the spring is taken to push back evenly, and the joint is taken to pass the spin on evenly. The model runs 8 times slower than the speeds shown, so that you can follow it.
