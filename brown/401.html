No. 401: Brownell's crank past the dead centre

A treadle turns a wheel through a rod and a crank pin. When the pin is in line with the rod, pushing the treadle cannot turn the wheel. Here the pin sits on a small slide, so the push moves the pin past that point first.

How it works

The rod joins the treadle to the red pin on the wheel. When the pin is at the very bottom, the rod points straight at the centre of the wheel. A push along it cannot turn the wheel either way. This is called a dead centre.

Here the wheel stops at that point each turn. The foot presses, and the rod pushes up on the pin. The wheel cannot turn, but the pin can still move sideways with the slide. The stops keep the slide from moving back, so it slides forward along its slots. Now the pin is past the dead centre, and the push turns the wheel. Then the spring pulls the slide back against its stops, ready for next time.

Turn the speed down and watch the readouts. The wheel reads 0 rpm while the slide moves out, then the wheel starts to turn.

In Brown's words

E. P. Brownell's patent crank-motion to obviate dead-centers. The pressure on the treadle causes the slotted slide, A, to move forward with the wrist until the latter has passed the center, when the spring, B, forces the slide against the stops until it is again required to move forward.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The bearing, the floor and its bracket, and all sizes were made up. The spring is drawn as a small coil round the hub with a wire to the slide. The model stops the wheel at the lower dead centre every turn, as if the treadle were worked very slowly; a running wheel would usually swing past by itself.
