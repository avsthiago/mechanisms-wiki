No. 490: Ship's steering gear

A hand wheel turns a barrel with a rope wound on it. The rope runs round two guide pulleys to the end of a long lever on the rudder. Turning the wheel swings the lever, and the rudder with it.

How it works

The wheel turns the barrel. One end of the rope winds onto the barrel and the other end unwinds from it. The rope runs from the barrel round a guide pulley to the end of the tiller. The other end goes round the other pulley to the same place.

So when one part of the rope is pulled in, the other is let out by the same amount. The pulled end drags the tiller towards its pulley, and the rudder turns. Turn the wheel back and the tiller swings back.

A thick barrel takes in more rope with each turn, so the rudder swings further. Try Thin, then Thick, and watch the tiller.

In Brown's words

Ordinary steering apparatus. Plan view. On the shaft of the hand-wheel there is a barrel on which is wound a rope which passes round the guide-pulleys and has its opposite ends attached to the "tiller" or lever on the top of the rudder ; by turning the wheel, one end of the rope is wound on and the other let off, and the tiller is moved in one or the other direction, according to the direction in which the wheel is turned.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which is a plan view, so the model is laid flat and seen from above. Brown does not show how it is held up. The floor, the posts under the bearings and pulleys, the rudder post and the wheel's spokes were made up. The wheel is turned back and forth, as a ship's wheel is, so the rope never runs out. The rope is treated as one that does not stretch.
