No. 260: Differential screw drive

A screw and its nut are both turned, each by its own gear, in the same direction. If they turn equally fast the screw stays put. When one turns a little faster, the screw creeps along very slowly and with great force.

How it works

The top shaft turns both big gears the same way. One gear holds the screw. The other gear holds the nut, so the nut spins round the screw.

A screw only moves through its nut when one turns faster than the other. If both turn equally fast, they turn together like one piece and the screw stays where it is. If the nut gains a little each turn, the screw moves by that little bit. So the screw moves by the difference in speed, not by the speed itself.

Try Equal and the screw stays still while everything spins. Then try Nearly equal and Far apart, and compare how fast the screw creeps.

In Brown's words

A differential movement. The screw, C, works in a nut secured to the hub of the wheel, E, the nut being free to turn in a bearing in the shorter standard, but prevented by the bearing from any lateral motion. The screw-shaft is secured in the wheel, D. The driving-shaft, A, carries two pinions, F and B. If these pinions were of such size as to turn the two wheels, D and E, with an equal velocity, the screw would remain at rest; but the said wheels being driven at unequal velocities, the screw travels according to the difference of velocity.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The sizes, tooth counts and thread spacing were made up. The base and the bracket at the back that holds the right end of the top shaft were added; Brown leaves that end loose. A screw can only go so far, so the model turns the driving shaft the other way at each end of its travel.
