No. 368: Drawing a spiral on a cylinder

One crank turns a cylinder and, at the same time, slides a marking point down beside it. The point draws a line that winds round the cylinder like the thread of a screw. Because one crank drives both, the turning and the sliding always keep in step.

How it works

Turn the crank and two things happen at once. The bevel gears turn the cylinder. The spur gear pushes the toothed bar down, and the marking point slides down the side of the cylinder.

Both movements come from the same crank, so they always keep in step. Each turn of the cylinder, the point drops by the same amount. That makes an even spiral, called a helix, like the thread on a screw. When the point reaches the bottom, the crank turns back and the point runs up the same line.

The bevel gears set how far the cylinder turns while the point moves down. Try Same as crank, then Twice as fast. With the cylinder turning faster, the line winds round more and lies flatter.

In Brown's words

Describing spiral line on a cylinder. The spur-gear which drives the bevel-gears, and thus gives rotary motion to the cylinder, also gears into the toothed rack, and thereby causes the marking point to traverse from end to end of the cylinder.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The legs, the floor bearing under the cylinder, the post behind the toothed bar, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. The second, smaller bevel gear is an extra to compare. The crank turns back on its own at each end so the model keeps going.
