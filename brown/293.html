No. 293: Duplex escapement

One wheel does two jobs here. Its long pointed teeth lean on a small roller on the balance and hold the wheel still. Its short pins give the balance a push, once for every swing there and back. It was used in watches.

How it works

The wheel is always trying to turn. The tip of a long tooth leans on the little roller, so the wheel cannot move. The roller turns to and fro with the balance and the tooth just slides on it.

The roller has a notch. When the balance swings anticlockwise the notch comes round, and the tooth slips into it and is carried through. The wheel is free and jumps forward. At once a pin on the wheel catches up with the pallet and pushes it along. That push keeps the balance swinging. Then the next long tooth lands on the roller and the wheel stops again.

On the swing back the notch meets the tooth going the other way. The tooth dips in a little and is pushed out again, so the wheel does not move on. That makes one step for every two beats. The balance spring sets the pace. Try Soft, then Stiff, and count the beats.

In Brown's words

Duplex escapement, for watches, so called from partaking of the characters of the spur and crown wheels. The axis of balance carries pallet, B, which at every oscillation receives an impulse from the crown teeth. In the axis, A, of balance-wheel is cut a notch into which the teeth round the edge of the wheel successively fall after each one of the crown teeth passes the impulse pallet, B.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which shows only the top of the wheel. The rest of the wheel, the balance wheel with its coiled spring, the back plate and the sizes were made up. The pallet is straight here and curved in the drawing. The notch was turned to the place on the roller where it has to be for the wheel to work. The mainspring is left out and its push on the wheel is taken to be steady. The tooth slides on the roller without rubbing, and only the balance and the wheel are slowed a little.
