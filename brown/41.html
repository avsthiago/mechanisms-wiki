No. 41: Gears with slanting teeth

Two gears with teeth cut on a slant pass turning from one shaft to the next. Each tooth slides into its partner a little at a time instead of all at once. That makes them run more smoothly and quietly than gears with straight teeth.

How it works

The upper gear turns and its teeth push the lower gear round the other way. Both gears are the same size, so they turn at the same speed. A minus sign in the readout means the other way.

Look at where the gears meet. A slanting tooth touches its partner at one end first. The touching spot then slides along the tooth to the other end. Before that tooth lets go, the next one has already started. So some tooth is always carrying the push. Gears like this are called helical gears. The slant has a cost: it also pushes each gear sideways along its shaft, so the shafts must be held firmly.

A straight tooth meets its partner along its whole length at once, with a small knock. Try Straight, then Slanting, and watch the red tooth go through the meeting place.

In Brown's words

Rotary converted into rotary motion. The teeth of these gears, being oblique, give a more continuous bearing than ordinary spur-gears.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows only the two gears on their shafts. The posts, the base, the sizes, the amount of slant and the number of teeth were made up. Brown draws both gears with teeth leaning the same way, but they must lean opposite ways to fit, so the model shows that. The straight teeth were added for comparison.
