No. 73: Spring ratchet drive

A wheel turns all the time, but the toothed wheel in front of it moves on by just one tooth each turn. A springy finger on the turning wheel is pressed into the teeth by a strong fixed spring. This gives a small, sure step once a turn.

How it works

The big disc turns all the time and carries the thin spring finger round with it. Most of the way round, the finger's hooked tip stays clear of the teeth. The ratchet wheel stands still.

On the left, the finger slides in under the strong spring. The strong spring is much stiffer, so it squeezes the finger's tip down into a gap between the teeth. The tip now pushes a tooth along, and the ratchet wheel turns with the disc. As soon as the tip gets past the end of the strong spring, the finger springs back out and the ratchet wheel stops. The end of the strong spring drops into the teeth and holds it there.

So the ratchet wheel moves one tooth for each turn of the disc. Try Backwards. The tip is still squeezed into the teeth, but now it slides over the slopes, and the ratchet wheel does not move at all.

In Brown's words

To the driving-wheel, D, is secured a bent spring, B; another spring, C, is attached to a fixed support. As the wheel, D, revolves, the spring, B, passes under the strong spring, C, which presses it into a tooth of the ratchet-wheel, A, which is thus made to rotate. The catch-spring, B, being released on its escape from the strong spring, C, allows the wheel, A, to remain at rest till D has made another revolution. The spring, C, serves as a stop.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The plate, the base, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. The end of the strong spring is bent in a little more than in the drawing, so it can rest in the teeth. The tip of the finger is wide, so the strong spring can press on it while the rest of the finger passes behind.
