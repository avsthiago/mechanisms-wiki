No. 503: Simple epicyclic bevel train

Two bevel gears face each other on one shaft, with a third gear between them on an arm. The arm always turns at the average of the two gears' speeds. Car axles use the same idea to let the two gears turn at different speeds in a bend.

How it works

The upper gear turns and the lower gear is held still. The side gear is squeezed between them. It rolls on the still gear and is pushed along by the moving one, so it travels round and takes the arm with it.

A rolling gear moves forward at half the speed of its top edge. In the same way, the arm goes round at half the speed of the upper gear. In general the arm's speed is halfway between the speeds of the two big gears.

Try Same way as the top. Both gears now agree, so the side gear stops spinning and everything turns together. Then try Other way. The two speeds cancel, the arm stands still, and the side gear just spins on it.

In Brown's words

A very simple form of the epicyclic train, in which F, G, is the arm, secured to the central shaft, A, upon which are loosely fitted the bevel-wheels, C, D. The arm is formed into an axle for the bevel-wheel, B, which is fitted to turn freely upon it. Motion may be given to the two wheels, C, D, in order to produce aggregate motion of the arm, or else to the arm and one of said wheels in order to produce aggregate motion of the other wheel.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not show what turns the two big gears; here they simply turn at the speeds you choose. The step the shaft stands on, the base, the collars, the pegs on the gears, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up.
