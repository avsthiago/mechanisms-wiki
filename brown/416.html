No. 416: Spring past the dead centre

A foot pedal, called a treadle, turns a wheel through a crank and a rod. Twice a turn the rod points straight at the shaft, and a push there does nothing. A spring pulls the crank away from those dead spots, so the treadle always starts.

How it works

The foot presses the treadle for a few turns, then rests. Watch where the crank stops each time. The foot can only push down, so it drives the wheel for half of each turn. The heavy wheel carries it through the other half.

When the crank pin is straight above or below the shaft, the rod pushes right at the shaft. That push cannot turn it at all. These are the dead spots. Without help, the weight of the treadle lets the crank settle in one of them, and then the foot is stuck. The cord pulls the pin to the left, so the crank comes to rest away from the dead spots.

When the crank rests at the side, a push on the treadle starts it at once. Try a weak spring and watch it stop close to a dead spot, or on the wrong side, where the foot cannot start it. Then try the spring as drawn.

In Brown's words

A device for assisting the crank of a treadle motion over the dead-centers. The helical spring, A, has a tendency to move the crank, B, in direction at right-angles to dead-centers.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the spring as a circle round a post with a line to the crank pin; here it is a coil spring that winds a barrel, and the line is a cord. The frame, the floor, the weights of the parts, the rub in the bearings and the foot's push were made up. The foot is not shown; it presses only while the treadle can go down.
