No. 72: Tilt hammer

A turning wheel with cams lifts a heavy hammer and lets it drop, again and again. Each cam gives one blow. Old forges used this, with a water wheel doing the work, to beat hot iron into shape.

How it works

The wheel turns clockwise. A cam slides under the lug and pushes it up. The arm rocks on its pin, so the head at the far end rises even higher than the lug.

When the tip of the cam slips past the lug, nothing holds the hammer up any more. It falls under its own weight and strikes the iron. With four cams, the wheel gives four blows in every turn.

Try two, four and six cams, and compare the blows with the wheel's turns. Then try six cams at top speed. Watch whether the hammer has time to fall all the way.

In Brown's words

Is a tilt-hammer motion, the revolution of the cam or wiper-wheel, B, lifting the hammer, A, four times in each revolution.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The exact shape of the cams, the post that holds the wheel's shaft, the pin and cheeks the arm rocks on, and the iron bar were made up. The hammer falls freely under its own weight, as a real one does. The choice of two or six cams was added to compare.
