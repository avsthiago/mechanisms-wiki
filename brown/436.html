No. 436: Jonval turbine

Water falls down a wide upright tube past a ring of fixed curved plates. The plates turn it sideways, and it pushes through the buckets of a wheel just below. The wheel and its shaft turn, and the shaft carries the power up.

How it works

Water runs in down the sloping pipe and falls inside the tube. First it goes between the fixed plates. They are curved, so the water comes out going round as well as down.

Then it falls through the buckets of the wheel, which bend the other way. Turning the water this way pushes the buckets round, and the wheel spins. The water drops out at the bottom with little speed left.

Try a low flow, then a high one. More water makes the wheel turn faster. Choose Closed to see the turbine as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Jonval turbine. The "shutes" are arranged on the outside of a drum, radial to a common center and stationary within the trunk or casing, b. The wheel, c, is made in nearly the same way; the buckets exceed in number those of the shutes, and are set at a slight tangent instead of radially, and the curve generally used is that of the cycloid or parabola.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which is a cut-away. The front half of the tube is left off so you can see in. The sizes, the pipe, the bearing at the bottom and the number and curve of the plates and buckets are made up. The wheel speed follows the flow by a simple rule.
