No. 46: Fusee and spring box

A coiled spring drives this watch. A spring pulls hard when it is wound tight and weakly when it is nearly run down. The cone, called a fusee, evens this out, so the watch gets the same turning force from start to finish.

How it works

First the watch is wound up. The fusee is turned quickly, and it pulls the chain off the spring box and up its spiral step. That turns the box and coils the spring tight. Then the spring pulls the chain back, and the fusee turns slowly the other way. This is the watch running.

A tight spring pulls hard, so the left bar is long. But the chain then leaves from the thin top of the fusee, close to the shaft. That is a short lever, so the hard pull turns the shaft only gently. As the spring runs down its pull fades, and the chain moves down to the wide part, which is a longer lever. Pull times lever stays the same, so the right bar does not move.

Try Plain drum. Now the lever never changes, so the turning force fades with the spring and the right bar shrinks. A watch built like that would not keep even time. Choose Closed to put the lid on the spring box, as in a real watch.

In Brown's words

Fusee chain and spring-box, being the prime mover in some watches, particularly of English make. The fusee to the right is to compensate for the loss of force of the spring as it uncoils itself. The chain is on the small diameter of the fusee when the watch is wound up, as the spring has then the greatest force.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The spring seen through the open top of the box, the base, the two force bars and the quick winding back up were added. The sizes and the force numbers are made up. Brown draws the fusee with even steps. Here its curve was worked out so the force stays even, so the steps get narrower towards the top. The wheels of the watch that the fusee drives are left out.
