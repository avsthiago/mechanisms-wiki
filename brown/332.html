No. 332: Side-lever parallel motion

In a side-lever engine a long lever rocks low down beside the cylinder, and a side rod joins its end to the top of the piston rod. The lever end swings along a curve, yet the piston rod must move in a straight line. A few light rods keep it straight.

How it works

Watch the end of the lever. It swings along part of a circle, and its grey trace bends. The cross-head at the top of the piston rod hangs on that end through the side rod, yet its red trace is a straight line.

The upright rod is moved at its foot by the lever, whose curve bends one way. Near its middle it is held by the radius bar, whose curve bends the other way. A point between them moves almost straight. The box of rods copies that point's motion to the cross-head, only larger.

The Off line reading shows how far the rod strays sideways, in millimetres. It stays tiny. Try a short swing, then a long one, and watch the worst error grow. Choose Closed to see the cylinder as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

A parallel motion used for the piston-rod of side lever marine engines. F, C, is the radius bar, and E the cross-head to which the parallel bar, E, D, is attached.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows one lever and one set of rods; a real engine has a pair, one each side of the cylinder. The shaft bearing, the post for the fixed pin, the stand under the cylinder and the floor were added. The cylinder is made a little deeper than drawn so the piston has room. The fixed pin was moved a little from where Brown drew it, so the line comes out as straight as it can. The lever is rocked steadily, as the engine's crank would rock it.
