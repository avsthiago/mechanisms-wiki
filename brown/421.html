No. 421: Trunk engine

A ship's engine that fits in a low space. A wide tube, called a trunk, is fixed to the piston and slides out through the cylinder top. The rod to the crank goes down inside the trunk and is pinned straight to the piston, so no long piston rod is needed.

How it works

Watch the piston. It slides up and down, and the trunk slides with it through the top of the cylinder. The rod inside the trunk leans one way, then the other, and turns the crank above.

The steam pushes on both sides of the piston in turn. Underneath, it pushes on the whole piston. On top, the trunk covers the middle, so the steam only pushes on the ring around it. To even this out, fresh strong steam goes on top first. Then it flows to the bigger space below, where it spreads out and pushes again.

Try a narrow trunk, then a wide one. Watch how much of the top of the piston is left for the steam to push on. Choose Closed to see the engine as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Trunk engine used for marine purposes. The piston has attached to it a trunk at the lower end of which the pitman is connected directly with the piston. The trunk works through a stuffing-box in cylinder-head. The effective area of the upper side of the piston is greatly reduced by the trunk. To equalize the power on both sides of piston, high-pressure steam has been first used on the upper side and afterward exhausted into and used expansively in the part of cylinder below.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front half of the cylinder, piston and trunk is cut away so the inside shows. The base, the post that holds the crank shaft, the shape of the crank and all the sizes were made up. The steam pipes and valves are left out; the blue dots show the steam. Dark dots are fresh steam pushing, and pale dots are used steam leaving.
