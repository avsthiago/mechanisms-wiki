No. 264: Two worm wheels on one worm

One worm turns two wheels of the same size side by side. One wheel has one tooth more than the other, so it turns a tiny bit slower. The two slowly drift apart, which is a way to count a very big number of turns.

How it works

Turn the worm and both wheels creep round the same way. Each turn of the worm pushes each wheel on by one tooth. At first the two pointers stand together.

One tooth is a smaller step on the wheel with more teeth, because its teeth are packed closer. So that wheel falls a little behind on every turn. The gap between the pointers grows by the same small amount each time. It takes as many worm turns as both tooth counts multiplied together for one wheel to gain a whole turn.

Try Same teeth and the pointers never part. Then try One tooth more and watch the gap grow. Look from the side to see the pointers.

In Brown's words

Two worm-wheels of equal diameter, but one having one tooth more than the other, both in gear with the same worm. Suppose the first wheel has 100 teeth and the second 101, one wheel will gain one revolution over the other during the passage of 100 x 101 teeth of either wheel across the plane of centers, or during 10,100 revolutions of the worm.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown uses wheels of 100 and 101 teeth; this model uses far fewer so the drift can be seen in minutes. The stands, the base and the sizes were made up. As Brown says, the wheels are the same size, so the teeth of the loose wheel are a little closer than the worm's thread. A real pair would cut the teeth to suit.
