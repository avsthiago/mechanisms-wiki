No. 491: Capstan

People push on long bars to turn a tall drum, and a rope wound round it is hauled in. A catch at the bottom lets the drum turn one way only. So the rope cannot pull the drum back when the pushing stops.

How it works

People push the bars round. The drum turns, and the rope winds on at its narrow middle. The loose end of the rope slides towards the drum, a little further with every turn.

At the bottom, the pawl is fixed to the drum and turns with it. Its tip rides up each sloping tooth of the ring, then drops down behind it with a click. A rope under a heavy load pulls the drum backwards, but the tip is stopped by the steep side of the tooth.

Try Backwards. The drum can only turn back as far as the nearest steep tooth side, and then it stops. Try Forwards again and the clicks start up.

In Brown's words

Capstan. The cable or rope wound on the barrel of the capstan is hauled in by turning the capstan on its axis by means of hand-spikes or bars inserted into holes in the head. The capstan is prevented from turning back by a pawl attached to its lower part and working in a circular ratchet on the base.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows one bar. The number of teeth, the floor, the size of the head and the way the pawl is hinged were made up. The rope is shown only on the narrow middle and a short loose end. The pawl drops to the bottom of each tooth at once, with no spring.
