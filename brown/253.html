No. 253: Centrifugal check hooks

A safety catch for the drum that winds a mine rope. If the drum ever spins too fast, hooks fly out from it and catch on fixed pegs. The drum stops before the load can crash down the shaft.

How it works

The drum winds the rope that lowers a cage or a load of ore. At normal speed the hooks stay folded against the disc. Their tips pass inside the pegs, so nothing touches.

Each hook is pulled in by a spring. As the drum turns, the hook is also flung outwards, like a stone whirled on a string. That outward pull grows fast: at twice the speed it is four times as strong. Above a certain speed it beats the spring, and the hook swings out.

A hook that is out reaches past the pegs. The next peg it meets lands in the crook of the hook, and the drum stops dead. Try normal speed, then running away, and watch which hook catches.

In Brown's words

Centrifugal check-hooks, for preventing accidents in case of the breakage of machinery which raises and lowers workmen, ores, etc., in mines. A is a frame-work fixed to the side of the shaft of the mine, and having fixed studs, D, attached. The drum on which the rope is wound is provided with a flange, B, to which the check-hooks are attached. If the drum acquires a dangerously rapid motion, the hooks fly out by centrifugal force, and one or other or all of them catch hold of the studs, D, and arrest the drum and stop the descent of whatever is attached to the rope. The drum ought besides this to have a spring applied to it, otherwise the jerk arising from the sudden stoppage of the rope might produce worse effects than its rapid motion.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows the hooks swung out; the folded position, the springs and the small stops were added to show how they are held in at normal speed. The rope ends, the sizes and the speeds are made up. Brown also says the drum should have a spring of its own to soften the jolt when it stops; that is left out, so here it stops at once.
