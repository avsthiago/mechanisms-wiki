No. 198: Mangle rack with a jointed rack

A small gear turns steadily in one place, yet the big frame slides to the right and then back to the left. The gear runs round the inside of a loop of teeth. At each end the loop is lifted or lowered on two swinging rods, so the gear's shaft never has to move.

How it works

A shaft from behind turns the small gear, called a pinion, in its place. It meshes with the teeth along the top of the loop and pushes the loop, and the frame with it, one way.

At the end of the loop the teeth curve round the pinion. The pinion pulls the loop upwards as it turns, and the two rods swing to let it rise. Now the pinion meshes with the bottom teeth, and the same turning pushes the frame the other way. The pinion never moves up or down. The rack does the climbing instead.

Try Clockwise, then Anticlockwise. Watch whether the rack is up or down while the frame moves to the right.

In Brown's words

A modification of 197. In this the pinion revolves, but does not rise and fall as in the former figure. The portion of the frame carrying the rack is jointed to the main portion of the frame by rods, so that when the pinion arrives at the end it lifts the rack by its own movement, and follows on the other side.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The rollers' supports, the board behind and all the sizes and numbers of teeth were made up. Brown draws an upright bar across the middle, joined to the rods. Here it is a strap fixed to the rack that holds the oval, because a bar joined to both rods would lock them. The two rods are made the same length so the rack stays level. The pinion's shaft goes back through a slot in the frame.
