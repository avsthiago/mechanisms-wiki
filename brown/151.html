No. 151: Worm and right and left screws

A small screw on the top shaft, called a worm, turns a gear, called a worm wheel. The wheel sits on a long shaft with a screw thread wound one way on its left half and the other way on its right half. Two blocks on the threads slide towards each other or away from each other, slowly and with great force.

How it works

Turn the crank end of the top screw, the worm. Its thread pushes the teeth of the wheel along, one tooth for each turn. So the wheel turns much more slowly than the worm.

The wheel is fixed to a long shaft, so the shaft turns with it. The left half of the shaft has a thread wound one way. The right half has a thread wound the other way. Each block, called a nut, cannot turn. The thread pushes it along the shaft. The two threads push in opposite ways, so the nuts move together or apart.

The worm is fast and the nuts are slow, so the nuts move a tiny amount for each turn of the worm. Try Together, then Apart, and watch the gap between the nuts.

In Brown's words

Continuous circular into continuous but much slower rectilinear motion. The worm on the upper shaft, acting on the toothed wheel on the screw-shaft, causes the right and left hand screw-threads to move the nuts upon them toward or from each other according to the direction of rotation.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The base, the posts at the ends of the long shaft, the plate that holds the worm, the rail that stops the nuts from turning, the crank disc and the sizes were made up. The wheel has plain straight teeth, as drawn. The nuts stop when they reach the end of the threads. In a real machine the worm would jam there.
