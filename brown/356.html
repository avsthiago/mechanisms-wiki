No. 356: Bohnenberger's machine

A heavy ball spins inside three rings, each free to turn inside the next. However the stand is turned or tipped, the axle of the spinning ball keeps pointing the same way. Ships and aircraft use the same idea to keep track of their direction.

How it works

Watch the red tip of the axle while the stand turns and tips under it. The stand and the outer ring go round. The tip does not follow. It keeps pointing at the same spot in the room.

The three rings turn on pivots set crosswise to each other. Between them, they let the stand lean or turn any way without twisting the ball. And a fast spinning ball holds its direction unless something twists it. So the rings swing round the ball, and the ball stays as it was.

Try Stopped. A ball that is not spinning has nothing to hold it steady. The small rub in the pivots is enough to carry it round with the stand. Then set it spinning again and see the tip hold still.

In Brown's words

Bohnenberger's machine illustrating the same tendency of rotating bodies. This consists of three rings, A, A', A², placed one within the other and connected by pivots at right angles to each other. The smallest ring, A², contains the bearings for the axis of a heavy ball, B. The ball being set in rapid rotation, its axis will continue in the same direction, no matter how the position of the rings may be altered ; and the ring, A², which supports it will resist a considerable pressure tending to displace it.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The tray, the way it is turned and tipped, and all the sizes were made up. The pivots are taken as perfectly free while the ball spins, so the axle does not drift at all. A real one drifts slowly. With the ball stopped, the rings are simply carried round with the stand. The ball is drawn turning much slower than it really does.
