No. 44: Stepped gears

Each gear is made of several thin gears fixed side by side, each turned a little from the one beside it, so the teeth make steps like a staircase. As a tooth on one thin gear lets go, a tooth on the next is already pushing, so the push never has a gap. Strong, smooth gears like this drove ships' propellers.

How it works

The upper gear turns and its teeth push the lower gear round the other way. Both gears are the same size, so they turn at the same speed. A minus sign in the readout means the other way.

Follow the red teeth. They do not reach the lower gear together. The one at one end arrives first, then its neighbour, then the next. Each thin gear takes up the push just after the one before it. So a fresh tooth is always coming into place, and the push stays steady.

With one plain gear, each tooth meets its partner along its whole length at once, with a small knock. Try 1, then 2, then 4, and watch how the red teeth arrive.

In Brown's words

A kind of gearing used to transmit great force and give a continuous bearing to the teeth. Each wheel is composed of two, three, or more distinct spur-gears. The teeth, instead of being in line, are arranged in steps to give a continuous bearing. This system is sometimes used for driving screw propellers, and sometimes, with a rack of similar character, to drive the beds of large iron-planing machines.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which shows four thin gears in each gear. The posts, the base, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. The choice of 1 or 2 thin gears was added for comparison.
