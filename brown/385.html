No. 385: Russian door closer

Two bars joined at the top hold up a weight above a door. Opening the door pulls the bottom ends of the bars closer, which lifts the weight. Let go, and the weight sinks and pushes the door shut.

How it works

When the door is shut, the two pins are far apart and the bars lean over low, like a flat roof. Open the door and its pin swings round towards the wall pin. The bars stand up steeper, and the weight rises.

Let go and the weight wants to sink. It can only sink if the bars lean over again. That pushes the two pins apart, and the only way they can move apart is for the door to swing shut. The pins turn in their sockets so the bars can follow the door round.

The wider the door opens, the higher the weight goes, and the harder it pushes back. Try 45°, then 120°, and compare how high the weight climbs.

In Brown's words

Contrivance employed in Russia for shutting doors. One pin is fitted to and turns in socket attached to door, and the other is similarly attached to frame. In opening the door, pins are brought together, and weight is raised. Weight closes door by depressing the joint of the toggle toward a straight line, and so widening the space between the pins.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows only the bars, the weight and the pins. The door, the wall, the sockets and all the sizes are made up. The door is pushed open for you, then let go. Its closing speed comes from the weight, the heaviness of the door and a little rub in the hinges.
