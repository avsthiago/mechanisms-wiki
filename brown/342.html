No. 342: Atmospheric beam engine

An old pumping engine that works by making a vacuum. Steam fills the cylinder, then a spray of cold water turns it back into water and leaves almost nothing behind. The air outside pushes the piston down, and the beam lifts the heavy pump rod at the other end.

How it works

First, steam at about the same pressure as the air is let in under the piston. Now the air pushes on the piston from above and the steam pushes from below, so they cancel. The heavy pump rod sinks and pulls the piston up.

Then a spray of cold water is shot into the cylinder. The steam turns back into a few drops of water and almost nothing is left under the piston. The air above is still pressing down, so it pushes the piston down hard. The beam rocks and lifts the heavy pump rod, and the pump lifts water out of the mine.

The curved heads keep both chains hanging straight down, so the rods go straight up and down. Watch the reading under the piston. Then turn the cold water spray off and see what happens. Choose Closed to see the cylinder and the pump as they look from outside.

In Brown's words

Old-fashioned single-acting beam pumping engine on the atmospheric principle, with chain connection between piston-rod and a segment at end of beam. The cylinder is open at top. Very low pressure steam is admitted below piston, and the weight of pump-rod, etc., at the other end of beam, helps to raise piston. Steam is then condensed by injection, and a vacuum thus produced below piston, which is then forced down by atmospheric pressure thereby drawing up pump-rod.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows only the cylinder end of the beam. The other end, the wall and its bearings, the pump, the weight, the pipes, the floor and all the sizes were made up, and so were the forces that set the pace. The steam valve and the water drain are left out. The cylinder is drawn see-through so the piston and the steam show.
