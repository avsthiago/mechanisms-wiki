No. 333: Special parallel motion

A beam rests on two swinging supports instead of a fixed pin. As it rocks, its far end slides along an almost straight line. That end can drive a piston rod with no other guide.

How it works

Watch the upper end of the beam. Its red trace is a straight slanting line, though no guide holds it. The lower end of the beam slides almost straight sideways on top of the rocking post.

The middle of the beam is about as far from the fixed pin as it is from each end of the beam. So the two ends and the fixed pin stay close to one circle round the middle. When one end slides along a line through that circle, the other end has to move along the line at right angles to it, through the fixed pin. The post's top swings on a curve, not a line, so the path is only nearly straight.

The Off line reading shows how far the rod strays from its line, in millimetres. Try a short swing, then a long one, and compare the worst error. Choose Closed to see the cylinder as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

A parallel motion used only in particular cases.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown says only that it is used in particular cases and does not say what drives it or what the lower rod and lever do. Here the radius link is rocked steadily, and the lower rod and lever simply follow. The small knob halfway down that rod is drawn as a collar. The rocking post's pin was moved a little and the beam's upper end made a little longer, so the line comes out as straight as it can. The cylinder, the floor and the bearings for the fixed pins were added. The drawing shows the beam at the top of its swing.
