No. 139: Sliding internal rack

A small gear turns steadily one way, yet the frame around it slides to the right and then back to the left. The gear runs along the bottom row of teeth, round the end of the toothed ring, and back along the top row. This makes a steady turning into a back and forth push.

How it works

Turn the crank and the small gear, called a pinion, turns with it. The pinion stays in one place. It meshes with the teeth along the bottom of the ring. The bottom of a clockwise gear moves left, so it pushes the ring and the frame to the left.

At the end of the ring the pinion rolls round the curved end. The ring slides up inside the frame, so the pinion comes to the top row. The top of a clockwise gear moves right, so now the frame goes right. The pinion never changes direction, but the frame goes out and comes back.

Try Clockwise, then Anticlockwise. See which way the frame moves while the pinion is on the bottom row.

In Brown's words

The internal rack, carried by the rectangular frame, is free to slide up and down within it for a certain distance, so that the pinion can gear with either side of the rack. Continuous circular motion of the pinion is made to produce reciprocating rectilinear motion of rectangular frame.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The crank, the plate behind, the base, the posts, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. Brown also draws two small levers and a bar across the top of the frame. They were left out, because here the teeth alone guide the ring up and down. The frame is taller than in the drawing, so the ring has room to slide. The teeth round the curved ends are spaced evenly along the loop, so they mesh well but are not exact gear teeth.
