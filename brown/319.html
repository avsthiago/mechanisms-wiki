No. 319: Compensation balance

The balance wheel of a good watch has a rim cut into two halves. Each half is made of brass and steel fixed together. When it gets warm the halves curl inwards, and the watch keeps the same time.

How it works

The wheel swings to and fro on its spring. Change the temperature and watch the free ends of the rim. When it is warm they curl inwards and bring the weights nearer the middle. When it is cold they open outwards.

Heat slows a plain watch. The wheel gets a little bigger and the spring gets a little weaker. A wheel with its weight far out is slow to turn, and one with its weight near the middle is quick. So pulling the weights in speeds the wheel up again. The brass outside grows more than the steel inside, and that bends each half inwards.

The real movement is far too small to see, and the model makes it much bigger. Try 5 °C, then 35 °C, and compare the plain wheel with this one.

In Brown's words

Compensation balance. t, a, t', is the main bar of balance, with timing screws for regulation at the ends. t and t' are two compound bars, of which the outside is brass and the inside steel, carrying weights, b, b'. As heat increases, these bars are bent inward by the greater expansion of the brass, and the weights are thus drawn inward, diminishing the inertia of the balance. As the heat diminishes, an opposite effect is produced. This balance compensates both for its own expansion and contraction, and that of the balance-spring.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The spring, the block that holds it, the back plate and the sizes were made up. The wheel swings far more slowly than a real one. The curling is made far larger than real so that you can see it. The readouts take a plain wheel and steel spring to lose 11 seconds a day for each degree warmer, and size the real curling to cancel that at the hot setting.
