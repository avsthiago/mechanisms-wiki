No. 250: Anti-friction wheels

A shaft rests on the rims of wheels instead of sliding in a plain bearing. The wheels turn with it, so almost nothing rubs. It makes heavy shafts turn easily.

How it works

The shaft does not rub on a bearing. It lies in the dip between two wheels and turns on their rims. As the shaft turns one way, the wheels turn the other way, the way two gears do.

The shaft is thin and the wheels are large. A point on the rim of the shaft travels exactly as far as a point on the rim of a wheel. So the wheels turn much more slowly than the shaft. The readouts show this.

Because the wheels roll under the shaft, the shaft does not slide over anything. Rolling wears things far less than sliding. Watch the red stripe on the disc and the red spoke on a wheel, and count how many turns of one match one turn of the other.

In Brown's words

Anti-friction bearing. Instead of a shaft revolving in an ordinary bearing it is sometimes supported on the circumference of wheels. The friction is thus reduced to the least amount.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows one bearing with a wheel pair on each side of the shaft and a large disc behind. The frame is drawn as two plain plates without the openings, and the wheels are placed side by side along the shaft so they can overlap as in the drawing without touching. The sizes, the disc and the red marks were made up.
