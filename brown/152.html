No. 152: Ellipsograph

A flat bar has two studs that slide in two grooves set at right angles, and a pencil fixed to the bar. As you turn the bar, the pencil draws an oval, called an ellipse. Draughtsmen used this tool to draw ovals that a compass cannot.

How it works

The two studs sit in two grooves that cross at a right angle. The bar has a fixed length between them. So when one stud slides toward the middle, the other must slide away from it.

The pencil is fixed to the bar, past one stud. As the bar turns, the studs slide to and fro in a straight line, and the pencil goes round in an oval. It is wide in the direction of the sideways groove and shorter in the upright one.

Try Close, then Far. The closer the pencil is to the stud, the flatter the oval gets.

In Brown's words

An ellipsograph. The traverse bar (shown in an oblique position) carries two studs which slide in the grooves of the cross-piece. By turning the traverse bar an attached pencil is made to describe an ellipse by the rectilinear movement of the studs in the grooves.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows two grooves on each arm. The model has one on each, which is all the studs need. The board under the cross-piece, the thickness of the plate and bar, and all the sizes were made up. The line is drawn a little above the board so that it can be seen. The other pencil positions were added for comparison.
