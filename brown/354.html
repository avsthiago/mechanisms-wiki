No. 354: Cross-head with a shaped groove

A crank turns a peg round in a circle. The peg runs in a looped groove cut in a cross-head, and the cross-head carries a rod up and down. The groove is shaped so the rod moves at one steady speed, instead of slowing down near each end.

How it works

The shaft turns, and the red peg goes round in a circle. The peg runs in a groove in the cross-head. The groove is a loop that bulges up on one side and down on the other, and crosses itself in the middle.

With a straight slot, the rod copies the peg's height. The peg climbs fast when it is level with the shaft, but hardly climbs at the top and bottom. So the rod speeds up and slows down. The shaped groove pushes the rod a little ahead or holds it back at each point. That makes the rod climb by the same amount for every bit the crank turns. It only stops for an instant to turn back.

Watch the rod speed. Try the shaped groove, then the straight slot, and compare how the speed changes.

In Brown's words

A modification of the crank and slotted cross-head, 93. The cross-head contains an endless groove in which the crank-wrist works, and which is formed to produce a uniform velocity of movement of the wrist or reciprocating-rod.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not give the shape of the groove, so it was worked out here: the loop is the path the peg takes when the rod moves at a steady speed. The guides, the plate behind them, the front bearing, the base and all the sizes were made up. The crank disc is drawn see-through so the peg can be seen. The straight slot was added for comparison.
