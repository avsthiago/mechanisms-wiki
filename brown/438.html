No. 438: Barker's mill

Water poured into a funnel runs down a hollow upright shaft and out of the ends of bent arms. As the jets shoot out one way, the whole shaft is pushed the other way and spins. It turns water falling from a height into a turning shaft.

How it works

Water poured into the funnel falls down the hollow shaft and into four arms. At the end of each arm the pipe bends, so the water sprays out sideways along the circle.

The water is pushed one way, so it pushes back on the arm the other way. That is called reaction. Each of the four arms is pushed round in the opposite direction to its jet, and the shaft spins on its point.

More water means stronger jets and a faster spin. Try a trickle, then a full pour, and compare how fast the red stripe goes round.

In Brown's words

Barker's or reaction mill. Rotary motion of central hollow shaft is obtained by the reaction of the water escaping at the ends of its arms, the rotation being in a direction the reverse of the escape.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The wall, its bracket, the block under the shaft and the chute are made up so the mill stands up. The water drops are simple dots that fall under their own weight. The way the shaft speeds up is simplified: the push from the jets is balanced by a small drag.
