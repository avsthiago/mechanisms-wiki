No. 7: Engaging, disengaging and reversing gear

One belt can turn an upright shaft one way, the other way, or not at all. You only slide the belt sideways onto a different pulley. The engine above never has to stop or change direction.

How it works

Three narrow pulleys sit side by side under the belt. Only the one under the belt gets pulled round. The fork slides the belt from one pulley to the next, while the top pulley keeps turning the same way.

The left pulley is fixed to a hollow tube, which carries a bevel gear. The right pulley is fixed to a thin shaft that runs inside the tube and carries a second bevel gear at the far end. These two gears face each other on either side of the top gear. So one turns the upright shaft one way, and the other turns it the other way.

The middle pulley is loose, so it spins alone and nothing else moves. Try Forward, then Loose, then Reverse, and watch the red pointer. A minus sign means a shaft turns the opposite way to the belt. The inner and hollow shafts always turn opposite ways.

In Brown's words

A method of engaging, disengaging, and reversing the upright shaft at the left. The belt is shown on the middle one of the three pulleys on the lower shafts, a, b, which pulley is loose, and consequently no movement is communicated to the said shafts. When the belt is traversed on the left-hand pulley, which is fast on the hollow shaft, b, carrying the bevel-gear, B, motion is communicated in one direction to the upright shaft; and on its being traversed on to the right-hand pulley, motion is transmitted through the gear, A, fast on the shaft, a, which runs inside of b, and the direction of the upright shaft is reversed.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing and description. The sizes, the tooth counts and the frame are made up. Brown does not show how the belt is moved, so the sliding fork was added. He does not say what the middle pulley turns on; here it is loose on the hollow shaft.
