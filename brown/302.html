No. 302: Balance-wheel escapement

A wheel with saw teeth round its rim is pulled round by a weight. Two small plates on a rocking rod catch its teeth in turn, so it moves one step at a time. A bar with a heavy ball on each end is fixed to the rod and slows the rocking down.

How it works

You are looking along the rocking rod. The weight pulls the crown wheel round, so the teeth nearest you move to the right and the teeth on the far side move to the left. A near tooth presses on the near pallet and pushes it aside. That turns the rod, and it swings the far pallet down into the teeth on the far side.

The near pallet swings up until its tooth slips under the end. The wheel jumps on, but at once a far tooth lands on the far pallet. That tooth pushes the rod back the other way. The heavy balance does not stop at once, so for a moment the pallet even pushes the wheel backwards. Each push and slip is one beat, and the wheel moves on one tooth for every two beats.

Nothing pulls the balance back to the middle. Only its weight sets the pace. With the balls far out, the balance is harder to turn, so the beats come further apart. Try Near the rod, then At the ends, and count the beats.

In Brown's words

Balance-wheel escapement. C is the balance ; A, B, are the pallets ; and D is the escape-wheel.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing and description. Brown shows the band of teeth from the side, the two pallets and the balance. The posts, the base and the sizes were made up, and the weight that turns the wheel is left out. The pallets are spread wider apart than Brown draws them, and the teeth are larger, with small flat tips, so that each tooth is caught in time. Sliding the balls along the bar is made up, though old clocks of this kind were set in a similar way. The touching of teeth and pallets is worked out in the flat view from the front. The parts slide on each other without rubbing, and the rubbing that keeps the swing steady is a simple guess.
