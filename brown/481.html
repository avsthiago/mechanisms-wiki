No. 481: Wet gas meter

This meter counts how much gas flows into a house. Inside a case half full of water, a drum with four curved rooms turns as the gas fills its rooms one after another. Each turn lets through the same amount of gas, so counting turns measures the gas.

How it works

The gas comes out of the pipe in the middle, just above the water. The door of one room is open to it there, and the gas fills that room. The darker grey shows fresh gas. The room's outer slot is still under water, so the gas cannot escape.

Gas is light and water is heavy. The gas trapped on one side of the drum floats that side up, so the drum turns. As the drum turns, that room's door dips under the water and shuts. Its slot comes up out of the water and the gas leaves, shown paler. Then the room sinks and fills with water again.

Each room holds a known amount of gas, so each turn of the drum is a known amount. A counter on the axle adds up the turns. Change the gas flow: the drum turns faster, but each turn still measures the same amount. Choose Closed to see the meter as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Wet gas meter. The stationary case, A, is filled with water up to above the center. The inner revolving drum is divided into four compartments, B, B, with inlets around the central pipe, a, which introduces the gas through one of the hollow journals of the drum. This pipe is turned up to admit the gas above the water, as indicated by the arrow near the center of the figure. As gas enters the compartments, B, B, one after another, it turns the drum in the direction of the arrow shown near its periphery, displacing the water from them. As the chambers pass over they fill with water again. The cubic contents of the compartments being known, and the number of the revolutions of the drum being registered by dial-work, the quantity of gas passing through the meter is registered.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The front of the case and drum is cut away. The shape of the walls, the slots, the outlet at the top, the feet and the amount of gas per turn are made up. The counter is left out; the readouts do its job. In a real meter the water inside a filling room sits a little lower than outside.
