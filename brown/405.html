No. 405: Drawing hyperbolas

A rule turning on a pin and a piece of cord draw a hyperbola, a curve with two open arms. The pencil keeps the cord tight against the rule. Then the rule is moved to the other pin to draw the facing curve.

How it works

The rule turns on the upper pin. The cord goes from the end of the rule, along its edge to the pencil, then to the lower pin. As the rule swings out, the pencil slides along it and draws the lower curve. Then the rule is turned over to draw the other half.

When the pencil moves away from the rule's pin, the cord along the rule gets shorter. All of that cord goes into the part between the pencil and the other pin. So the two distances grow by the same amount, and their difference never changes. That is what makes the curve a hyperbola.

Next the rule and the cord swap pins, and the upper curve is drawn the same way. Try a short cord, then a long one, and watch the difference in the readouts and the shape of the curves.

In Brown's words

Mechanical means of describing hyperbolas, their foci and vertices being given. Suppose the curves two opposite hyperbolas, the points in vertical dotted center line their foci. One end of rule turns on one focus as a center through which one edge ranges. One end of thread being looped on pin inserted at the other focus, and other end held to other end of rule, with just enough slack between to permit height to reach vortex when rule coincides with center line. A pencil held in bight, and kept close to rule while latter is moved from center line, describes one-half of parabola ; the rule is then reversed for the other half.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's description. The paper, the length of the rule and the cord lengths are made up. Brown draws a thread tied at the rule's end; here it lies along the edge of the rule. The rule is lifted off its pin to turn it over and to move it to the other pin. Brown calls the curve a parabola in one place, but the tool draws a hyperbola.
