No. 315: Conical pendulum

A pendulum that goes round in a circle instead of swinging to and fro. Its rod sweeps out a cone. A clock driven this way runs smoothly, with no tick at all.

How it works

The driving shaft turns the arm. The arm pushes the rod round, and the rod leans out and sweeps a cone. The heavy weight, called the bob, goes round in a level circle. It never stops and turns back, so nothing ticks.

Going round in a circle needs a pull towards the middle. The leaning rod gives it: the weight hangs from the wire, and the lean turns part of that pull inwards. Faster turning needs more inward pull, so the rod leans further and the bob climbs. A real pendulum about as long as a ruler would have to turn about once a second.

Below a certain speed the rod hangs straight. Just above it, a small change in speed makes a big change in lean. Move the speed from the slowest to the fastest and watch the cone open up.

In Brown's words

Conical pendulum, hung by a thin piece of round wire. Lower end connected with and driven in a circle by an arm attached to a vertical rotating spindle. The pendulum-rod describes a cone in its revolution.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The bars of the arm are made up, so that the rod can slide out as it leans further; Brown does not show how the rod's end is held. The block on the floor and the sizes were made up, and the clock wheels that drive the bevel gear are left out. The lean is worked out for a pendulum as long as a ruler, treating the rod as weightless. The speeds are only those at which the rod still reaches the arm.
