No. 194: Mangle wheel with one ring of teeth

A small gear turns steadily one way, yet the big wheel turns most of a turn one way and then back again. It moves at the same speed both ways. Mangles, machines that press wet washing between rollers, used this to roll the cloth to and fro.

How it works

Turn the crank and the small gear, called a pinion, turns with it. In the picture its teeth catch the pins from outside the ring. It pushes the wheel round the opposite way to its own turning.

At the gap the pinion swings round the last pin and goes inside the ring. Now it pushes the pins from inside, so the wheel turns back. Both ways it uses the same pins, at the same distance from the middle, so the wheel moves just as fast each way. A peg behind the pinion runs in the groove, and the guide lets the shaft rise and fall.

Watch the wheel speed readout as the pinion goes round the gap. The wheel slows, stops and turns back. A minus sign means the other way.

In Brown's words

Another mangle-wheel. In this the speed is equal in both directions of motion, only one circle of teeth being provided on the wheel. With all of these mangle-wheels the pinion-shaft is guided and the pinion kept in gear by a groove in the wheel. The said shaft is made with a universal joint, which allows a portion of it to have the vibratory motion necessary to keep the pinion in gear.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The crank, the guide, the jointed shaft, the post, the base, the sizes and the number of pins were made up. Brown draws long slots for the teeth. Here they are shorter, so the pinion can swing round the last one at each end. Brown says the shaft has a universal joint; here it has two, so the pinion stays square to the wheel and turns at the same speed as the crank.
