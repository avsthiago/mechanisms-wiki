No. 226: Speeding up with equal bevel gears

Six bevel gears, all the same size, make one gear turn faster than the shaft that drives it. A frame carries one gear round the driven shaft, and that gear spins on its own pin as it goes. The two motions add up.

How it works

Turn the driving shaft. Its gear drives two gears on the driven shaft, one on each side of it, so they turn opposite ways. The right one is fixed to the driven shaft. The left one is on a hollow shaft that turns loosely round the driven shaft.

The frame is fixed to the driven shaft and carries the middle gear round with it. That alone would take the end gear round once for each turn of the driving shaft. But the hollow shaft turns the other way, so its gear also spins the middle gear on its pin. That spin turns the end gear twice more.

So the end gear makes three turns for each turn of the driving shaft. Take the hollow shaft out and hold the middle gear still, and the end gear only goes round with the frame. Try both and compare the readouts.

In Brown's words

This movement is designed to double the speed by gears of equal diameters and numbers of teeth—a result once generally supposed to be impossible. Six bevel-gears are employed. The gear on the shaft, B, is in gear with two others—one on the shaft, F, and the other on the same hollow shaft with C, which turns loosely on F. The gear, D, is carried by the frame, A, which, being fast on the shaft, F, is made to rotate, and therefore takes round D with it. E is loose on the shaft, F, and gears with D. Now, suppose the two gears on the hollow shaft, C, were removed and D prevented from turning on its axis; one revolution given to the gear on B would cause the frame, A, also to receive one revolution, and as this frame carries with it the gear, D, gearing with E, one revolution would be imparted to E ; but if the gears on the hollow shaft, C, were replaced, D would receive also a revolution on its axis during the one revolution of B, and thus would produce two revolutions of E.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown says the end gear turns twice for each turn of the driving shaft. Following the gears through gives three turns: one with the frame, and two from the spin of the middle gear. So it turns twice as fast as the frame that carries it, and three times as fast as the shafts. The bearings, the stand, the pin through the driven shaft, the crank on the end gear, the hand wheel and the number of teeth were made up.
