No. 222: Eccentric gear and linked pinion

A round gear turns on a shaft that is off its centre, so it wobbles. A small gear, called a pinion, rides on a swinging arm and stays in mesh with it, held by a link to the gear's centre. The pinion drives a second gear that turns round at a changing speed.

How it works

The driving gear is round, but its shaft is not at its centre. As it turns, its centre goes round in a small circle and its teeth come nearer and further. The pinion has to follow. It rides on an arm that swings about the driven gear's shaft.

A link joins the pinion's centre to a pin at the centre of the driving gear. The link never changes length, so the two gears always stay the right distance apart. The teeth cannot go too deep or slip apart. When the far side of the gear meets the pinion, its teeth move fast and the driven gear hurries. On the near side it lags.

Move the shaft off the centre slider. With Centred, the driven gear turns at a steady speed. Try A little, then As drawn, and watch the Driven readout swing more.

In Brown's words

If for the eccentric wheel described in the last figure an ordinary spur-gear moving on an eccentric center of motion be substituted, a simple link connecting the center of the wheel with that of the pinion with which it gears will maintain proper pitching of teeth in a more simple manner than the groove.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The plate, the shafts, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up. The slider was added. It moves the shaft's place in the gear to show what the offset does.
