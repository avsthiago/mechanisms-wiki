No. 22: Pulleys each hung from its own rope

Several pulleys hang one above the other, each in its own loop of rope. Every pulley halves the pull, so three of them let you lift eight times what you pull. In return, the weight rises very slowly.

How it works

Look at the lowest pulley. It hangs in a loop of rope. One end is tied to the beam and the other to the pulley above. So two ropes hold it up, and each carries half the weight.

The pulley above does the same. It only has to hold that half, and its own two ropes share it again. With 3 pulleys, 100 kg becomes 50, then 25, then 12.5 kg at your hand. That is 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 times less.

Now watch the speeds. Each pulley rises half as fast as the one above it. Pull in 8 cm of rope and the pulleys rise 4 cm, 2 cm and 1 cm. Try 2 pulleys, then 4, and compare how far the weight moves.

In Brown's words

Are different arrangements of pulleys. The following rule applies to these pulleys:—In a system of pulleys where each pulley is embraced by a cord attached at one end to a fixed point and at the other to the center of the movable pulley, the effect of the whole will be = the number 2, multiplied by itself as many times as there are movable pulleys in the system.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which shows 3 moving pulleys; the control also lets you try 2 or 4. Brown leaves the end of the rope hanging; here it runs straight down to a winch. The winch, the frames round the pulleys, the rings and the weight are made up. Real pulleys rub on their pins and have weight of their own, so a real pull is a little more.
