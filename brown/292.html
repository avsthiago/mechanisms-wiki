No. 292: Stud escapement

This escapement was made for big clocks, such as the clock in a tower. Small studs stand out from both faces of a wheel. Two pallets on a swinging frame, one in front of the wheel and one behind, let the studs past one at a time.

How it works

The wheel is always trying to turn. A stud runs up against the curved face of a pallet and rests there, so the wheel cannot move.

The pallets swing with the pendulum. Each curved face is part of a circle drawn round the pin at the top. So the pallet can slide along under a resting stud without pushing the wheel back or letting it creep forward. When the end of the face slides out from under the stud, the stud runs down the sloping end and pushes the pallet aside. That push keeps the pendulum swinging. The wheel moves on a little, until a stud on its other face lands on the other pallet.

The pendulum sets the pace. A long pendulum swings more slowly, so the beats come further apart and the wheel turns more slowly. Try Short, then Long, and count the beats.

In Brown's words

Stud escapement, used in large clocks. One pallet, B, works in front of the wheel and the other at the back. The studs are arranged in the same manner, and rest alternately upon the front or back pallet. As the curve of the pallets is an arc described from F, this is a repose or dead-beat escapement.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which shows part of the wheel and the two pallets. The rest of the wheel, the pendulum, the back plate and the sizes were made up. The studs are half-round here. The pendulum hangs in front so that it can be seen. The weight that turns the wheel is left out and its pull is taken to be steady. The studs slide on the pallets without rubbing, and only the pendulum and the wheel are slowed a little.
