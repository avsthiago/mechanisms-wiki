No. 204: Rolling contact on oblique shafts

Two rollers shaped like cotton reels sit on shafts that cross at a slant without meeting. They touch along a straight line, so one can turn the other with no teeth at all. Rollers like this join shafts that point different ways.

How it works

Turn the upper roller and the lower one turns the other way. Their shafts cross at a slant and never meet. Yet the two rollers touch all along one straight line, from end to end.

That is possible because of their shape, called a hyperboloid. It looks curved, but it can be made entirely of straight lines laid at a slant round the shaft. The red stripe is one of those lines. Where the rollers touch, one straight line of each lies along a line of the other. Rubbing at that line drives the lower roller.

When the rollers are the same size, both turn at the same speed. Pick Different sizes. Now the thin roller turns nearly twice as fast as the thick one. Watch how often each red stripe comes round.

In Brown's words

An illustration of the transmission of rotary motion from one shaft to another, arranged obliquely to it, by means of rolling contact.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows only the two rollers and their shaft ends. The rings and posts that hold the shafts, the base, the angle between the shafts and the sizes were made up. The rollers here are true hyperboloids, so they touch along a whole straight line. Real rollers like these also slide a little along that line as they turn, which wears them. The choice of different sizes was added to show the change in speed.
