No. 495: Entwistle's gearing

Three equal bevel gears make a drum turn twice as fast as the shaft that drives it. One gear is fixed and never turns. A second gear is carried round it by the shaft and rolls on it as it goes.

How it works

Turn the hand wheel. The shaft carries the middle gear round in a circle. That gear touches the fixed gear, which cannot turn, so the middle gear has to roll on it and spin on its own pin.

The drum gear touches the other side of the middle gear. It gets one turn from being carried round with the shaft. It gets one more turn from the spin of the middle gear. The two add up, so the drum makes two turns for each turn of the shaft.

It also works backwards. Turn the drum instead, and the shaft goes round at half the drum's speed, but with twice the push. Try The shaft, then The drum, and compare the readouts.

In Brown's words

Entwistle's patent gearing. Bevel-gear, A, is fixed. B, gearing with A, is fitted to rotate on stud, E, secured to shaft, D, and it also gears with bevel-gear, C, loose, on the shaft, D. On rotary motion being given to shaft, D, the gear, E, revolves around A, and also rotates upon its own axis, and so acts upon C in two ways, namely, by its rotation on its own axis and by its revolution around A. With three gears of equal size, the gear, C, makes two revolutions for every one of the shaft, D. This velocity of revolution may, however, be varied by changing the relative sizes of the gears. C is represented with an attached drum, C'. This gearing may be used for steering apparatus, driving screw-propellers, etc. By applying power to C, action may be reversed, and a slow motion of D obtained.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows it cut through the middle; here the gears are whole. The base, the shape of the two bearings, the stripe on the hand wheel and the number of teeth were made up.
