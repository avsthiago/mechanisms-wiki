No. 313: Spring-detent chronometer escapement

This is the escapement of a ship's chronometer, a very exact clock once used to find a ship's place at sea. A small stop on a springy lever holds the escape wheel. The balance frees it for a moment, takes one push, and then swings on its own.

How it works

The escape wheel is always trying to turn. One tooth rests on the small stop on the lever, so the wheel cannot move. For most of its swing the balance touches nothing at all.

When the balance swings anticlockwise, the little stud near its axis catches the end of the passing spring and presses it against the hook. The lever has to move too, and the stop lets go of its tooth. The wheel jumps forward. Another tooth, waiting beside the big roller, runs into the block on the roller and gives the balance a push. The lever springs back in time to catch the next tooth.

On the swing back the stud only bends the passing spring away from the lever and slips past. Nothing is let go, so the wheel moves one tooth for every two beats. The balance spring sets the pace. Try Soft, then Stiff, and count the beats.

In Brown's words

Chronometer escapement, the form now commonly constructed. As the balance rotates in the direction of the arrow, the tooth, V, on the verge, presses the passing-spring against the lever, pressing aside the lever and removing the detent from the tooth of the escape-wheel. As balance returns, tooth, V, presses aside and passes spring without moving lever, which then rests against the stop, E. P is the only pallet upon which impulse is given.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The balance wheel with its coiled spring, the back plate, the number of teeth and the sizes were made up. The lever is straight here and bowed in the drawing, and the passing spring points straight at the axis of the balance. Both springs are shown as stiff strips on hinges, the lever at its foot and the passing spring at the hook, instead of bending along their length. The mainspring is left out and its push on the wheel is taken to be steady. The parts knock and slide on each other without rubbing, and only the balance and the wheel are slowed a little.
