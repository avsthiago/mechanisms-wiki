No. 212: Geneva stop

A stop that lets a watch spring be wound only so many turns, then blocks the key. A wheel with one finger turns a star wheel one step per turn. One side of the star is left uncut, and that side is what stops it.

How it works

Turn the key and the round disc goes with it. Once each turn its finger slips into a slot of the star wheel. It pushes the star round by one slot, then slides out. In between, the round edge of the disc sits in a hollow of the star and holds it still.

One side of the star has no hollow. It is left as a full round edge. When that side has to swing past the disc, it hits the disc's edge and cannot go on. So the key stops after a few turns, and the spring cannot be wound too tight.

Let it run and watch the turns count up until the key stops. Then it turns back the other way, as the spring unwinds, until the uncut side stops it again from the other side. Count the turns between the two stops.

In Brown's words

What is called the "Geneva-stop, used in Swiss watches to limit the number of revolutions in winding-up; the convex curved part, a, b, of the wheel, B, serving as the stop.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The key, the plate that holds the shafts and the sizes were made up. The hollows, the slots and the cut-backs beside the finger were worked out so the finger moves the star exactly one slot and nothing rubs. The model stops at the exact place where the uncut side first touches the disc. The spring itself is left out, so the model just turns back to show unwinding.
