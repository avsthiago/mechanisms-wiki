No. 15: White's pulleys

Two blocks, each with one wheel cut with three grooves of different sizes. One rope winds back and forth between them, so six ropes share the weight. The grooves are sized so each wheel turns as one piece and the rope never rubs.

How it works

Count the ropes between the two blocks. There are six, and all six hold up the lower block. So the winch only has to pull a sixth of the weight.

Now lift the weight a little, say 1 cm. Every rope between the blocks must get 1 cm shorter. Start at the tied end. The first rope needs 1 cm, so 1 cm of rope runs round the first bottom groove. The next rope needs its own 1 cm as well, so 2 cm run over the first top groove. Then 3, 4, 5 and 6 cm. That is why the winch pulls in 6 cm.

A groove twice as big carries twice as much rope in one turn. So grooves sized 1, 3 and 5 below, and 2, 4 and 6 above, let each wheel turn as one solid piece with no rubbing. Try loose pulleys: they are all the same size, so each one has to spin at its own speed.

In Brown's words

Represents what are known as White's pulleys, which can either be made with separate loose pulleys, or a series of grooves can be cut in a solid block, the diameters being made in proportion to the speed of the rope; that is, 1, 3, and 5 for one block, and 2, 4, and 6 for the other. Power as 1 to 7.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown gives the power as 1 to 7. Counting the ropes in his drawing, six hold up the lower block, which gives 1 to 6, and that is what the model shows. Brown leaves the end of the rope hanging; a small winch was added to pull it. The weight, the sizes and where the rope's end is tied are made up. Real wheels still rub a little on their pins.
