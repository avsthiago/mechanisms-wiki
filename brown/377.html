No. 377: Tread-mill

A person climbs steps on the outside of a big drum. Their weight turns the drum, like a person on a never-ending staircase. A gear on the shaft passes the turning on to a machine, such as a pump or a mill.

How it works

The person stands on one board and holds the rail. Their weight pushes the board down, so the drum turns and the next board comes up under their other foot. They step up all the time but stay in the same place.

The turning force depends on where they stand. At the very top their weight pushes straight at the shaft and turns nothing. The further round the front they stand, the more their weight pulls the drum round. The big gear has twice as many teeth as the small one, so the small gear turns twice as fast.

Try standing near the top, then well down the side, and watch the turning force. Then change the drum speed and see how fast the feet have to move.

In Brown's words

The tread-mill employed in jails in some countries for exercising criminals condemned to labor, and employed in grinding grain, etc.; turns by weight of persons stepping on tread-boards on periphery. This is supposed to be a Chinese invention, and it is still used in China for raising water for irrigation.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The shaft, the two bearings, the base and the small output gear were made up. So were the slanted posts that hold the rail, and the sizes of the drum, boards and gears. The person is a simple blocky figure. They walk steadily, one step for each board, and stay where you place them.
