No. 444: Hydraulic ram

Water running down from a tank is stopped suddenly, again and again. Each time, its push forces a little water up into a vessel full of air. The squeezed air throws a jet higher than the tank it all came from, with no pump and no engine.

How it works

The tank stands above the basin. Water runs down the long pipe and out through the waste valve on the right. It goes faster and faster. When it runs fast enough, it drags the waste valve up and slams it shut.

The moving water in the pipe cannot stop at once. It pushes on, opens the other valve and forces some water into the air vessel. That squeezes the air. Then the water stops, bounces back a little, and the weight drops the waste valve open again. A new beat starts.

The squeezed air pushes water up the thin pipe all the time, so the jet stays steady. Its top is higher than the water in the tank. Try a light weight, then a heavy one, and watch the beats and the jet. Choose Closed to see the ram as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Montgolfier's hydraulic ram. Small fall of water made to throw a jet to a great height or furnish a supply at high level. The right-hand valve being kept open by a weight or by spring, the current flowing through the pipe in the direction of the arrow escapes thereby till its pressure, overcoming the resistance of weight or spring, closes it. On the closing of this valve the momentum of the current overcomes the pressure on the other valve, opens it, and throws a quantity of water into the globular air-chamber by the expansive force of the air in which the upward stream from the nozzle is maintained. On equilibrium taking place, the right-hand valve opens and left-hand one shuts. Thus, by the alternate action of the valves, a quantity of water is raised into the air-chamber at every stroke, and the elasticity of the air gives uniformity to the efflux.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The pipes and valve box are drawn square, cut down the middle, and the valve guide, the chute and the sizes of the parts are made up. The motion is worked out from the fall, the length of the pipe and the air pressure in the vessel. Real water stops in a pressure wave much faster than shown; here the water in the pipe is treated as one solid lump.
