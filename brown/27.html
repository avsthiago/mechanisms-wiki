No. 27: Multiple gearing

A small driver with three arms has a roller on each arm. The rollers slide in grooves cut across a bigger wheel and push it round. The big wheel turns exactly half as fast, and it needs no teeth.

How it works

Watch the red roller. As the driver turns, the roller slides along its groove, through the middle of the wheel and out to the far edge. Then it slides back again. It never leaves that groove.

A roller can only slide along its groove, so as it goes round it has to push the groove sideways. That push turns the wheel. Each roller's circle passes right through the middle of the wheel. Because of that, the wheel turns once for every two turns of the driver, at a steady speed.

A push at the very middle cannot turn a wheel, so a roller does no work as it crosses there. The other two rollers are far out in their grooves at that moment and keep the wheel going. Change the speed and check that the wheel always shows half the driver's speed.

In Brown's words

"Multiple gearing"—a recent invention. The smaller triangular wheel drives the larger one by the movement of its attached friction-rollers in the radial grooves.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown gives no sizes. The distance between the two shafts was chosen so that each roller passes exactly through the middle of the wheel, which gives the steady half speed. The small knob Brown draws at the middle of the wheel was left out so the rollers can pass. The stand was made up.
