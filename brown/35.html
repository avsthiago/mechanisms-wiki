No. 35: Elliptical gear with a sliding pinion

A small gear turning at a steady speed drives a big oval gear. The oval gear speeds up and slows down twice in every turn. A spring and a slotted bar let the small gear move in and out to follow the oval.

How it works

Turn the crank and the small gear, called a pinion, turns at a steady speed. Its teeth push the big oval gear round. Watch the red stripe on the oval gear. It hurries, then lags, twice in every turn. A minus sign in the readout means the other way.

The pinion moves the big gear's teeth past it at a steady rate. At a long end of the oval, those teeth are far from the big gear's shaft, so the gear only turns a little. At a flat side the teeth are close to the shaft, so the same push turns the gear further. That is why it speeds up and slows down.

The edge of the oval moves in and out as it turns, so the pinion has to move with it. Its shaft slides along the slot in the yellow bar, and the spring pulls it in to keep the teeth together. Slow the speed right down. Watch the pinion slide in the slot while the readout rises and falls.

In Brown's words

Variable rotary motion produced by uniform rotary motion. The small spur-pinion works in a slot cut in the bar, which turns loosely upon the shaft of the elliptical gear. The bearing of the pinion-shaft has applied to it a spring, which keeps it engaged; the slot in the bar is to allow for the variation of length of radius of the elliptical gear.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown does not show what turns the pinion, or what stops the loose bar from swinging right round. Here a crank turns the pinion, and its shaft slides between two fixed rails on the back plate. The crank, the rails, the plate, the sizes and the number of teeth were made up.
