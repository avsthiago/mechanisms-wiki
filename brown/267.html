No. 267: Friction pulley with eccentric arms

A pulley rim that drives its shaft one way only. Turned one way, four arms inside it jam against the rim and the shaft turns with it. Turned the other way, the arms slip and the shaft stays still.

How it works

The rim turns loose on the shaft. The hub is fixed to the shaft. Four arms hang from pins at the corners of the hub, and springs press their curved ends onto the inside of the rim.

Each arm leans back a little from straight out. When the rim turns against the arrow, it drags the arm's end forward. That tries to stand the arm up straighter, which would need more room than there is. So the arm jams tight, and the rim carries the hub and shaft round with it. When the rim turns with the arrow, it drags the arm's end back. That only tips the arm further over, so it cannot jam. The rim slides past and the shaft stays still.

Try against the arrow, then with the arrow, and watch the Shaft reading. The arms grip one way and slip the other, so the shaft only ever turns one way.

In Brown's words

Friction pulley. When the rim turns in the opposite direction to the arrow, it gives motion to the shaft by means of the pivoted eccentric arms ; but when it turns in the direction of the arrow, the arms turn on their pivots and the shaft is at rest. The arms are held to the rim by springs.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The shapes of the arms and hub follow the drawing loosely. The arms' working ends are shaped so that they lean back from the rim by the same small angle wherever they touch, which is less than the angle at which they would slide. The springs, the web behind the rim, the bearing and the base were made up. When the arms slip, a load on the shaft is taken to hold it still.
