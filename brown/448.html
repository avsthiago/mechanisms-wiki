No. 448: Common lift pump

The pump you work by hand in an old farmyard. Each time the handle goes down, a piston inside the tube lifts the water above it, and some pours out of the spout. Two little valves make sure the water only ever goes up.

How it works

Watch the piston as it rises. The lid on top of it is pressed shut, so it lifts all the water above it. Below it, the bottom valve opens and water from the well rushes up to fill the space. When the water above reaches the spout, it pours out.

On the way down, the bottom valve drops shut, so no water goes back to the well. The lid on the piston lifts instead, and the piston slides down through the water. Nothing pours out on this stroke. The water stays where it is, ready to be lifted again.

The water below is pushed up by the air pressing on the well, not pulled. That push can only hold up about 10 m of water. Try 4 m, then 12 m, and watch the tube stay empty. Choose Closed to see the pump as it looks from outside.

In Brown's words

Common lift pump. In the upstroke of piston or bucket the lower valve opens and the valve in piston shuts ; air exhausted out of suction-pipe, and water rushes up to fill the vacuum. In down-stroke, lower valve is shut and valve in piston opens, and the water simply passes through the piston. The water above piston is lifted up, and runs over out of spout at each up-stroke. This pump cannot raise water over thirty feet high.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing, which is a cut-away. The front half of the pump is left off so you can see in. The sizes, the shape of the valves and the made-up size used for the litres are not from the book. The pipe down to the well is cut short, and the model starts with it already full of water.
