No. 167: Drum with an endless spiral groove

An upright drum has one groove that runs all the way round it. Half the groove slants up and half slants down. A stud on a sliding block rides in the groove, so the block goes up and down once for every turn of the drum. Brown says this was used in place of a crank in a steam engine.

How it works

Watch the red stud. The drum turns, and the groove slides past it. On the front of the drum the groove slants down to the right. On the back it slants up again. The wall of the groove pushes the stud, and the stud pushes the block.

The groove has no end. Where the downward slope finishes, it turns round and starts the upward slope. So the drum can keep turning the same way while the block goes up, then down, then up again. Each trip takes half a turn.

It also works the other way. If the block is pushed up and down, the stud pushes on the groove walls and spins the drum. That is how it could replace a crank. Try Short, Medium and Long. A longer trip means the block must go faster to finish it in the same turn.

In Brown's words

A drum or cylinder having an endless spiral groove extending all around it; one half of the groove having its pitch in one, and the other half its pitch in the opposite direction. A stud on a reciprocating rectilinearly moving rod works in the groove, and so converts reciprocating into rotary motion. This has been used as a substitute for the crank in a steam engine.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The top and bottom plates, the shaft, the upright rod, the sliding block and the stud were made up. So were the drum's size and the depth and width of the groove. Brown shows only a thin line for the groove. Here it is cut into the drum so the stud can sit in it. The groove is shaped so the block moves at a steady speed, with a short smooth turn at each end. The choice of how far the block slides was added.
