No. 45: Frictional grooved gearing

Two wheels with no teeth turn each other just by pressing together. Their rims are cut into ridges and grooves that fit into each other. The sloping sides wedge tight, so they grip much harder than flat rims would.

How it works

The top wheel turns at a steady speed. Its rim presses on the rim of the bottom wheel, and the rub between them turns that wheel too. The two wheels turn opposite ways. A minus sign in the readout means the other way.

Look at the wheels from the front. The rims are cut into V-shaped rings, and each ridge sits in a groove of the other wheel. When the wheels are pressed together, the sloping sides squeeze into each other like a wedge. That squeeze makes the rub much stronger than on flat rims, so the wheels slip less. The smaller wheel turns faster, because a turn of its rim covers less ground.

Try Pulled apart, then Pressed together. Apart, the top wheel spins alone and the bottom wheel stops. There are no teeth, so only the pressing makes it work.

In Brown's words

Frictional grooved gearing—a comparatively recent invention. The diagram to the right is an enlarged section, which can be more easily understood.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. Brown shows two wheels edge on, the bottom one a bit larger, and a close-up of the ridges fitting together. The plate that holds the shafts, the number and depth of the ridges and the exact sizes were made up. The choice to pull the wheels apart was added to show that only the pressing makes them drive.
