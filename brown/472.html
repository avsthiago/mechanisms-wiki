No. 472: Grimshaw's air hammer

A small hammer worked by squeezed air instead of steam. A pump fills the hollow frame with air. A sliding valve sends that air above or below the hammer's piston in turn, so the hammer rises and is driven down.

How it works

The pump on the left squeezes air into the hollow frame. The frame keeps it at a steady push, like a tank. On top of the hammer's cylinder, a small valve slides back and forth as the shaft turns.

When the valve slides one way, air from the frame goes under the piston and lifts the hammer. The air above escapes up the short pipe. When it slides back, air goes on top and drives the hammer down, while the air below escapes. The hammer falls faster than by its own weight.

Try low air, then high air. With too little push the hammer can hardly lift itself. With more, it rises quickly and lands much harder. Choose Closed to see the cylinder and pump as they look from outside.

In Brown's words

Grimshaw's compressed air hammer. The head of this hammer is attached to a piston, A, which works in a cylinder, B, into which air is admitted like steam to a steam engine above and below the piston by a slide-valve on top. The air is received from a reservoir, C, in the framing, supplied by an air pump, D, driven by a crank on the rotary driving-shaft, E.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The cylinder and pump are cut open at the front. The valve rods and the bracket that turns their motion round a corner, the shaft brackets, the pipes and all sizes were made up. The frame is drawn as a flat plate behind the moving parts. The air from the frame reaches the valve box straight through the back of the box, and the anvil is set a little lower than drawn so the hammer has room to swing. The motion is worked out from the air pushing on each side of the piston and the hammer's weight, slowed down. The blue dots show the air.
