No. 446: Oscillating water column, cone formed

Water piles up into a cone on a round plate until it plugs the hole the water falls from. The moving water above has nowhere to go, so it rises above the reservoir. Then the cone bursts and the beat starts again.

How it works

This is the moment the cone of water on the plate reaches up into the hole. It is tall and narrow, and runs on up inside the small tube. The stream stops falling. Only a thin sheet still runs off the edge of the plate.

The water in the long channel was moving, and it cannot stop at once. It runs on into the small tube and heaps up there, higher than the water in the channel. It splashes up out of the top. The column keeps rising until its weight bursts the cone. Then the stream falls again, faster than before, and the column sinks.

Time runs slowly here, because this moment is short. Watch the Column reading climb while the Cone reads its tallest. Try a big supply, then a small one, and see how often the cone forms. Choose Closed to see the tubes as they look from outside.

In Brown's words

D'Ectol's oscillating column, for elevating a portion of a given fall of water above the level of the reservoir or head, by means of a machine all the parts of which are absolutely fixed. It consists of an upper and smaller tube, which is constantly supplied with water, and a lower and larger tube, provided with a circular plate below concentric with the orifice which receives the stream from the tube above. Upon allowing the water to descend as shown in 445, it forms itself gradually into a cone on the circular plate, as shown in 446, which cone protrudes into the smaller tube so as to check the flow of water downward ; and the regular supply continuing from above, the column in the upper tube rises until the cone on the circular plate gives way. This action is renewed periodically and is regulated by the supply of water.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawings and description. The tubes are drawn square and cut down the middle, and the stem under the plate is made up. How fast the cone grows, and how much it can hold back before it bursts, are chosen so the beat works; Brown gives no figures. The rise and fall of the water in the channel are worked out from its weight and speed. The shape of the cone and the splash at the top of the small tube are drawn to look like Brown's figures.
