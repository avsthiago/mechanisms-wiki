No. 474: Hero's aeolipile

A hollow ball spins on two pipes above a pot of boiling water. Steam fills the ball and rushes out of bent arms, and the ball turns the other way. It was made in ancient Egypt more than 2000 years ago, and is often called the first steam engine.

How it works

The fire boils the water in the closed pot. The steam goes up the two pipes and into the ball. Its only way out is through the bent arms, so it shoots out of their ends in four jets.

When a jet of steam is pushed out of an arm, the arm is pushed back the other way. This push is called reaction. All four jets point round the same way, so all four pushes turn the ball the opposite way. The ball speeds up until the air and the rubbing on the pipes hold it back as hard as the jets push.

Watch it start from still as the steam builds up. Then try a low fire and a high fire, and compare how fast the red ring goes round.

In Brown's words

Æolipile or Hero's steam toy, described by Hero, of Alexandria, 130 years B.C., and now regarded as the first steam engine, the rotary form of which it maybe considered to represent. From the lower vessel, or boiler, rise two pipes conducting steam to globular vessel above, and forming pivots on which the said vessel is caused to revolve in the direction of arrows, by the escape of steam through a number of bent arms. This works on the same principle as Barker's mill, 438 in this table.

This model is rebuilt from Brown's drawing. The fire under the pot, the number of arms and all the sizes were made up. The steam numbers are only a guide. The way the ball speeds up is simplified: a steady push from the jets against a drag that grows with speed.
